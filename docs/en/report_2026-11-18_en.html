Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral summit in Shenzhen

⌄

Coverage of the planned Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral summit in Shenzhen is broadly skeptical across sources, but with sharply different emphases. The dominant worry, voiced across several outlets, is the exclusion of Ukraine and Europe from talks that could decide their fate. At The Guardian, Rafael Behr, in his column, develops the sharpest version of this argument, writing that the summit flatters Putin's vanity by granting him symbolic parity with genuine superpowers and risks letting him "pour poisonous theories" into Trump's ear about the war's origins, while Europe is left to choose between deeper continental cooperation and self-defeating nationalist fantasies like Brexit; a companion piece by Behr and a shorter teaser on the same theme restate this warning rather than adding a new angle. Tom Tugendhat, in his column in The Daily Telegraph, goes further still, invoking "Yalta 2.0" to warn that a West that has failed to invest in defence, especially Britain and Europe, risks having its fate decided by others' communiqués, and calls for new Western structures like CANZUK and the Joint Expeditionary Force. By contrast, two letters to the Los Angeles Times dismiss the summit's substance altogether: one calls it a mere "photo op" built on Trump's mistaken belief that Xi and Putin are his friends while China's rise and Russia's war grind on regardless; the other, by Josh Kiok, draws a historical parallel to the 1938 Munich Conference, suggesting Ukraine's future may be settled without it present just as Czechoslovakia's was. German-language sources offer more analytical treatments of the power balance. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in a piece by Majid Sattar, Friedrich Schmidt and Jochen Stahnke, frames the meeting as largely symbolic, with China seeking prestige and leverage without real arms-control concessions, Putin pushing maximalist demands on Ukraine, and smaller nations fearing a "new Yalta." The same paper's "Stimmen der anderen" section relays The Times's assessment that a genuine reset of world order is unlikely despite both Trump and Putin being weakened, though modest pragmatic gains on energy or trade are possible. In his column in Die Welt, Gregor Schwung describes Trump arriving in Asia as a "lame duck" after a heavy midterm defeat, arguing Asian governments will test his weakness and the Xi meeting will likely expose a less favorable balance of power for Washington. Süddeutsche Zeitung, in a piece by Lea Sahay, pushes a similar argument further, contending that despite superficial Yalta comparisons, only Xi truly controls the summit's direction, since Trump has weakened his own hand and Putin remains dependent on Beijing, which seeks images of power rather than real deals. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in a piece by Isabelle Jacobi, takes a more cautious, open-ended approach, stressing uncertainty over the agenda (Ukraine, nuclear arms control, Iran) alongside views that dialogue itself has value even absent concrete deliverables. The Guardian, in a separate piece by Rebecca Bailey, shifts focus to the parallel Apec summit, portraying Xi as using the stage-managed event to project China as multilateralism's steward and an equal to the US. USA Today, reported by Francesca Chambers and Miles Herszenhorn, takes a more domestic American angle, covering Trump's China trip with Elon Musk in tow and quoting skepticism that the new trilateral format, now including Xi as Putin's key ally, will yield better results. Finally, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in a separate piece by Till Fähnders, broadens the lens to Trump's visit to the Philippines and ASEAN, noting that Southeast Asian states welcome his engagement but fear a US-China "G2" arrangement carving the world into spheres of influence, a sentiment echoing the broader warning that those not seated at the table may end up on the menu.

Kerch Bridge strike and Russia-Ukraine war fallout

⌄

Coverage of the Kerch Bridge strike is broadly unanimous that it was a heavy symbolic blow to Putin, but outlets differ in emphasis. The Daily Telegraph, The Globe and Mail, The Guardian and Los Angeles Times offer largely factual reporting on the "unprecedented" operation, as Zelensky called it, folding it into wider context of Russian strikes on Kyiv and ongoing peace talks; The Globe and Mail and Los Angeles Times, in a shared dispatch by Hanna Arhirova and Barry Hatton, stress that the strike capped weeks of preparatory attacks. The Guardian provides the most analytical treatment: in one piece Luke Harding frames the timing as deliberately designed to embarrass Putin ahead of his summit with Trump and Xi, while in a separate column Dan Sabbagh argues the strike demonstrates Kyiv's growing long-range firepower and Russia's vulnerability. German-language coverage leans more interpretive: in a column for Die Welt, Clemens Wergin argues the strike undermines Putin's control over Crimea and cripples southern logistics, and in a separate piece he describes how an alternative Israeli satellite system is letting Ukraine hit Russian refineries more effectively. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung runs straightforward news coverage of the strike itself alongside a column by Nikolas Busse contending that Russia, not Zelensky, remains the real obstacle to a negotiated peace, plus a reprinted outside view (from Der Standard) calling Putin's readiness to talk a sham. Süddeutsche Zeitung, in Florian Hassel's report, frames the strike as both strategically and symbolically damaging, while also noting Russia's retaliatory strikes on Kyiv bridges. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in a piece by Volker Pabst, strikes a more cautious note, acknowledging the blow to Putin's prestige project while pointing out that experts disagree over the bridge's real remaining military value. This produces a split between English-language outlets emphasizing Ukraine's immediate symbolic victory and some German-language analysis adding caveats about the strike's practical military significance.

COP31 climate conference in Antalya

⌄

Coverage of the COP31 summit in Antalya splits along diplomatic, economic and technological lines. The Guardian concentrates on climate finance and political hypocrisy: in a report by Damien Gayle, Fiona Harvey and Graham Readfearn it notes that the UK has backed international levies on polluting sectors to fund climate action, while in a separate piece by Fiona Harvey and Simon Evans it reveals that Russia, Saudi Arabia and Australia are history's top CO2 exporters, arguing that wealthy fossil-fuel exporters wield disproportionate political power at the talks. A third Guardian piece widens this into a Global South perspective, arguing that mechanisms such as the Loss and Damage Fund are grossly inadequate against the debt burdens of countries like Nepal, and that the international economic system perpetuates colonial-era inequalities. By contrast, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung focuses on the diplomatic mechanics themselves: in a piece bylined simply "itz.", it argues that the official negotiating draft is contradictory and unrealistic, while informal coalitions such as TAFF and the 35-by-35 initiative are gaining momentum outside official channels. In the same newspaper, Joachim Müller-Jung adds a distinct technological angle, warning in his article that AI chatbots readily abandon evidence-based climate facts under pressure from repeated false claims, especially in non-Western languages, a danger he frames as especially pressing amid the summit's disinformation concerns. Süddeutsche Zeitung, through an interview conducted by Christoph von Eichhorn with IPCC chair Jim Skea, offers a sober scientific assessment: Skea states that the 1.5-degree target is likely no longer attainable, that countries remain divided over the urgency and timing of future reports, and that adaptation measures lag far behind what is needed, even as carbon removal technologies draw growing attention. Together, these sources show The Guardian foregrounding economic responsibility and inequality between rich and poor nations, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung dwelling on negotiation dynamics and information risks, and Süddeutsche Zeitung providing a critical scientific stocktake of the conference's overall prospects.

EU migration policy and Rwanda return hubs

⌄

Coverage of the EU plan to set up Rwanda-based "return hubs" for rejected asylum seekers ranges from detailed institutional reporting to sharp ideological critique. Die Welt offers the most extensive coverage: one article by Ricarda Breyton and Nikolaus Doll outlines the plan by Germany, Austria, Denmark, the Netherlands and Greece to establish such hubs and its legal basis, while a separate Die Welt article focuses on the internal German coalition dispute over Interior Minister Dobrindt's proposal, noting that the SPD and the justice ministry are skeptical of its effectiveness and that NGOs warn Germany risks legitimizing Rwanda despite its role in fueling violent conflict in eastern Congo. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung presents two contrasting angles: a factual report by Alexander Haneke covers record irregular migration numbers in the Canary Islands and the intentions of five EU states to strike a deal with Rwanda, leaving open the question of what interest the authoritarian Rwandan government has in cooperating, while a separate item under the paper's "Stimmen der anderen" rubric relays the Spanish newspaper El País's argument that the agreement represents a genuine triumph for xenophobic populism, which now dictates policy without needing to win elections, and condemns the dehumanizing treatment of asylum seekers. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in an article by Sebastian Briellmann, adds a Swiss angle, reporting that the EU's new return regulation permits return hubs in third countries like Rwanda even without migrants having any personal connection there, and that Switzerland must adopt this law within two years, with the Swiss government cautiously open yet skeptical given a history of unfulfilled tough migration pledges. Die Welt additionally broadens the German domestic migration context: another Breyton article covers Hesse's push to speed up deportations of foreign criminals, while, in a personal column, Gideon Böß argues that modern communication, travel technology and welfare benefits have fundamentally transformed migration itself, turning it from a permanent rupture into something closer to an elaborate "move." Overall, Die Welt concentrates on German political and administrative detail, Neue Zürcher Zeitung examines the broader European legal dimension and its implications for Switzerland, and Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung combines factual reporting with a sharply critical ideological voice against the policy as a whole.

Höcke/AfD historical revisionism on WWII

⌄

All three German newspapers cover Björn Höcke's Volkstrauertag speech and the broader pattern of historical revisionism it represents, but each foregrounds a different angle. In his piece for Die Welt, Frederik Schindler focuses on fact-checking Höcke's rhetoric, showing in detail how the AfD leader inflates German casualty figures from Allied air raids to cast Germans as victims while minimizing Nazi crimes, citing historians who reject his numbers. European sovereign debt and political volatility

⌄

In his column in The Daily Telegraph, Russ Mould takes the broader, more alarmist view, arguing that upcoming elections in Spain, France, Germany and Poland threaten to amplify political volatility, and that combined with high debt levels in France and Italy, the eurozone's lack of fiscal union, and its inability to devalue, Europe could be heading toward a fresh debt crisis, this time centered on the bloc's core members rather than its periphery.
