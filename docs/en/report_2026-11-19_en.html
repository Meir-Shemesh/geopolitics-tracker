Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral summit in Shenzhen and 'new Yalta' fears

⌄

Coverage of the unprecedented Trump-Xi-Putin summit in Shenzhen splits largely between those invoking the "new Yalta" framing and those rejecting it. The Daily Telegraph, in Adrian Blomfield's report, presents the summit through the eyes of apprehensive US allies in Europe and Asia who fear Trump's embrace of Putin and Xi signals a return to great-power carve-ups of spheres of influence. Die Welt, in a piece by Sebastian Beug and Sophia Cai, similarly surveys Yalta comparisons but balances them with expert voices dismissing the idea of spheres-of-influence politics as outdated nineteenth-century thinking. Neue Zürcher Zeitung pushes back most explicitly: in a signed analysis, Markus Ackeret argues the Yalta comparison is misleading, since Yalta followed a clear military outcome at the end of a war, whereas today there is no victor in either the Ukraine or Middle East conflicts and the three leaders' interests are unequal and divergent. In an opinion piece, The Guardian likewise denies the meeting constitutes genuine diplomatic progress comparable to Yalta, describing it instead as spectacle without a real agenda that sidelines smaller nations. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung runs an opinion roundup juxtaposing The Wall Street Journal's warning that Xi and Putin could use nuclear talks as cover for abandoning Ukraine and emboldening China over Taiwan, against the Frankfurter Rundschau's view, cited there, that Shenzhen is no new Yalta and need not worry Europe. Separate Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung reports focus on the choreography of the summit, China's warm treatment of Putin contrasted with coolness toward Western-aligned leaders, and the applause Putin received from Western and Chinese business leaders despite ordinary citizens' skepticism about economic conditions. Süddeutsche Zeitung, across two reports including one by Daniel Brössler, emphasizes the practical agenda of nuclear arms reduction and US pressure on Putin via Xi regarding Ukraine, while noting Ukrainian fears of being sidelined in talks over its own fate. The Washington Post, in three pieces, strikes a particularly skeptical note: one, by Joshua Keating and Cate Cadell, highlights China's reluctance on denuclearization and irritation at Trump's push; another argues Trump's nuclear-deal dream collides with unwilling Russian and Chinese partners; a third, by Joshua Yang, offers historical context on Xi's family ties to Shenzhen as emblematic of China's economic transformation. USA Today, in two reports, likewise stresses that the summit is likely to yield mostly symbolism given deep disagreements over Ukraine, Iran, trade and China's military buildup. The Globe and Mail and Los Angeles Times lean toward straightforward reporting of the leaders' warm reception and dynamics, with the Los Angeles Times piece by Michael Wilner adding analysis of each leader's diverging goals: Putin seeking leverage on Ukraine, Xi positioning China as a global power and peace broker, and Trump balancing engagement with hardened trade measures. Finally, a separate Guardian report by Rebecca Bailey focuses on Xi casting China as defender of free trade and multilateralism against new US chip export restrictions.

Future of the Democratic Party ahead of 2028

⌄

Five outlets approach the future of the Democratic Party from strikingly different angles, with the underlying tension being whether the party's path forward lies with the ideological left or the pragmatic center. The Guardian, in a report by Alice Speri, documents the organized push within the DSA and the broader US left to draft Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez for a 2028 presidential bid, framing it as evidence that democratic socialism is moving into the mainstream. In his column in The Washington Post, Henry Olsen offers a different analytical lens, arguing the eventual nominee will likely emerge from the "progressive uniter" quadrant and suggesting a Black candidate such as Kamala Harris, Wes Moore, or Raphael Warnock may be best positioned given Black voters' historical preferences. Finally, in her column for USA Today, Sara Pequeño narrows the debate to Senate leadership, arguing Chris Van Hollen offers a bolder and more trustworthy alternative to Chuck Schumer. Together, these five pieces reveal a far wider conversation than any single one captures, spanning socialist momentum, demographic calculation, international image-building, and internal leadership battles.

UN Cop31 climate summit

⌄

Coverage of the Cop31 climate summit in Antalya converges on a sense of deadlock and obstruction but differs in focus and depth. The Guardian, in reporting by Fiona Harvey, Damien Gayle and Graham Readfearn, centers on the anger of developing-country ministers and campaigners, who accuse rich nations and fossil fuel interests of holding the world hostage to a "petroleum mafia" and offering only "crumbs" in climate finance, with the core fight over fossil fuel phase-out still unresolved.
