Russia-Ukraine war peace negotiations and European security order

⌄

Coverage of the emerging Russia-Ukraine peace talks splits along several distinct fault lines. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung and Süddeutsche Zeitung focus heavily on Europe's internal fragmentation over representation: pieces by Thomas Gutschker and by Daniel Brössler with colleagues describe the rivalry between EU Council President Costa and the "E3" (Germany, France, Britain) over who speaks for Europe, warning this undermines the bloc's credibility. The Washington Post, Los Angeles Times and USA Today report more straightforwardly on the practical outcome - a five-nation team (Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Poland) plus EU officials - emphasizing Poland, the Baltic and Nordic states' fears of being sidelined by a US-Russia deal over Europe's head. Die Welt takes a different internal-EU angle, centering on Chancellor Merz's threat to tie EU budget approval to "fair" burden-sharing on Ukraine aid, a standoff Christoph B. Schiltz calls a dead end; Süddeutsche Zeitung's Hubert Wetzel and Daniel Brössler cover the same clash but add sharper commentary, Wetzel warning of Merz's risky gamble and Brössler, in his column, defending Merz against Slovak premier Fico's accusations of blackmail. A third cluster - Jacques Schuster's column in Die Welt, Friedrich Schmidt's reporting in Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, the Globe and Mail's dispatch, and two commentaries in Neue Zürcher Zeitung by Inna Hartwich and Antonio Fumagalli - converges on the claim that Russia's demands for a NATO rollback to 1997 borders and a "new security architecture" simply repeat its 2021 maximalist ultimatum aimed at dismantling NATO and redividing Europe; Hartwich goes furthest, framing this as a calculated "divide and rule" strategy exploiting the remainder of Trump's term. The Guardian offers the widest lens: Jennifer Rankin quotes Kaja Kallas insisting the aggressor has no legitimate security concerns, while Patrick Wintour and an unsigned companion piece push the analysis toward a potential Trump-Putin-Xi "Yalta 2.0," a great-power carve-up risking abandonment of small states like Ukraine and Taiwan - a global-order framing absent from the other sources.

COP31 climate summit in Antalya

⌄

Coverage of the COP31 climate summit in Antalya splits into several distinct angles. The Globe and Mail and USA Today, in a jointly-bylined report by Sarah McFarlane and Valerie Volcovici, both focus on the core policy clash: UN Secretary-General António Guterres's call for immediate, drastic emissions cuts against resistance from major fossil fuel producers and consumers such as the United States, Saudi Arabia, Russia and India. The Guardian, in a piece by Fiona Harvey, Oliver Milman and Graham Readfearn, extends the same Guterres speech into a broader warning about climate change's link to global instability and peace, urging his successor not to downgrade the issue. Several columnists offer sharper, more interpretive critiques. In a column in Die Welt, Marion Sendker highlights the gap between Turkey's self-promotion as a climate leader hosting the summit and its actual environmental record, while in a separate Die Welt column Kristina Schröder argues that Europe's unilateral climate ambition is largely self-damaging as long as other economies free-ride on its efforts, calling instead for a "climate club" with minimum carbon pricing. In a column in Süddeutsche Zeitung, Thomas Hummel attacks Germany and other countries for hypocrisy in blocking real climate action despite available clean-tech solutions, while in another Süddeutsche Zeitung column Christoph von Eichhorn contrasts Greta Thunberg's angry rhetoric about a corrupt "global regime" unfavorably with Al Gore's more constructive messaging at the same conference. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung adds two further angles: a report on the Fridays for Future protests and Thunberg's surprising distancing from the movement, and a piece by Anna Nowaczyk on Nepal's push for greater climate-damage funding after a deadly glacial flood, noting the EU's reluctance and its pointing to China and Saudi Arabia instead. In a column in The Daily Telegraph, Trevor Jones mocks the British prime minister's claim to climate leadership as hypocritical, arguing he should have addressed the summit by video link rather than flying to Turkey. The Los Angeles Times, across three pieces by Jennifer Dlouhy plus a related one by Gustavo Arellano on US-Mexico relations, centers on Gavin Newsom's sharp criticism of Trump's absence from the summit, which he argues cedes global influence to China and harms US competitiveness.

Neue Zürcher Zeitung likewise offers two angles: Michael Rasch chronicles Huang's final days atop Nvidia while linking the personal scandal to the broader US-China tech rivalry, and a separate piece widens the lens to growing concern over Chinese espionage reaching into Switzerland itself.

US-Iran conflict: fallout and ongoing risks

⌄

Coverage of the lingering US-Iran confrontation splits into several complementary strands. Die Welt offers a straight factual account that Iran has told the IAEA it will not resume cooperation on nuclear inspections under the threat of military strikes, quoting the agency's warning that the lack of information on Iran's nuclear sites is a "cause for concern." In a column for Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Friederike Böge analyzes the rise of Mohsen Rezaei as the regime's leading voice, stressing his threats against US bases in Europe and his refusal to surrender enriched uranium, which she reads as evidence Tehran distrusts Trump's claims the war is over. Writing for Neue Zürcher Zeitung, Christian Weisflog argues that despite Republican midterm losses and Trump's own acknowledgment that the war hurt his party, the president remains determined to force Tehran's regime to its knees, with blockade, sanctions and military posturing likely to drag on for years. The Guardian, in Patrick Wintour's report, centers on direct military escalation: Iran's claim to have destroyed a US radar system in Bahrain in retaliation for an American strike, alongside British public opposition to the war and Iran's move to review its nuclear non-proliferation commitments. Los Angeles Times contributes a distinct intelligence angle - Nabih Bulos reports how Iran exploited data harvested from soldiers' social media, fitness apps and Russian satellite imagery to precisely target US bases, inflicting heavy casualties, while a companion piece details the Navy's resulting push to tighten cyber security. Finally, three outlets - Globe and Mail, The Guardian (via Lauren Almeida) and Neue Zürcher Zeitung - shift to the economic fallout, all reporting, based on the same IEA assessment, that global oil supply has swung back into surplus for the first time since the war began as Gulf exports recovered despite continued blockade and tanker attacks, with Neue Zürcher Zeitung additionally noting a forecast of further recovery through 2027.

German auto industry crisis amid China and US competition

⌄

All eight pieces agree that the German car industry is in deep trouble, but they diverge sharply on cause and cure. Writing in The Daily Telegraph, Matthew Lynn takes the most radical line, treating Mercedes-Benz's investment freeze as proof that Germany's entire consensual, export-driven economic model is finished and must be overhauled through lower energy costs, weaker union power and less bureaucracy. Die Welt offers two complementary angles: in an interview conducted by Christoph Kapalschinski, the head of Aumovio calls for cutting labour costs and "de-engineering" German products to compete with China, while taking a relatively welcoming view of Chinese manufacturers setting up in Europe; a separate, unsigned Die Welt report describes the squeeze from both Chinese technological advances and US tariffs, and gives voice to union figure Barbara Resch, who accuses employers of neglecting Germany as a production location and misleading workers with skewed wage comparisons. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung likewise presents two angles: in a signed column, Armand Zorn argues that the debate over combustion-engine bans is a distraction and that Germany and Europe should instead invest in batteries, semiconductors and autonomous driving while imposing tariffs on Chinese hybrids; an unsigned companion piece focuses narrowly on Mercedes, arguing that collapsing Chinese demand combined with Chinese automakers dumping overproduction into Europe has ended the company's comfortable cross-subsidy model, forcing painful cuts at home. Neue Zürcher Zeitung's Peter A. Fischer broadens the lens to European industry as a whole, framing it as a genuine "second China shock," but rejects subsidies and tariffs in favour of renewed investment, automation and training. Süddeutsche Zeitung concentrates on the domestic labour conflict at Mercedes: one report treats the investment freeze and plant-closure threats as a factual, nationally significant escalation with IG Metall preparing strikes, while an unsigned companion piece strikes a more even-handed note, arguing both sides are mutually dependent and that their escalating confrontation is self-destructive and urgently needs compromise.

Espionage threats: NATO, Switzerland and Germany vs Russia/China
