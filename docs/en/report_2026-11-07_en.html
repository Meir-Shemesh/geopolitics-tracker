Wave of bomb threat hoaxes across Europe

⌄

While all the sources describe the same wave of bomb-threat hoaxes disrupting flights and broadcasters across Europe, they differ in how confidently they link it to Russia. The Daily Telegraph, in Emily Smith's report, centers on the threatening message itself - an anonymous group vowing to be "blowing up your airplanes, cruise ships, subway stations, and trains" - and leaves the identity of the perpetrators as an open question for investigators. The Globe and Mail and the Los Angeles Times, sharing a jointly bylined report by Stanislav Hodina, Karel Janicek and Geir Moulson, stress that authorities have not formally attributed the threats to any actor, but note that the hoaxes follow other disruptions - attacks on supermarket refrigeration systems and drone incursions - suggesting a broader pattern of hybrid threats. Süddeutsche Zeitung, in Jens Flottau's account, takes a more measured, technical approach, reporting 18 concrete threats against flights and noting that the Kremlin itself dismissed suggestions of Russian involvement. By contrast, USA Today and The Guardian adopt a more assertive tone: USA Today quotes officials declaring flatly "we are under attack all the time," aimed at destabilizing European societies that support Ukraine, while The Guardian, in a piece by Rory Carroll and Jakub Krupa, cites an official assessment that "the most reasonable assumption is that we are facing an attempt at hybrid aggression and a form of psychological warfare," floating possible involvement by both Russia and Iran. The most unequivocal voice comes from the Polish press: Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung reprints a column from the Polish magazine Polityka arguing forcefully that Russia "is waging an intensive hybrid war against Europe," linking the bomb threats to GPS jamming along NATO's borders as part of a deliberate campaign to destroy trust in institutions.

Crackdown on democracy activists in Hong Kong and Macau

⌄

All four sources document the same wave of repression through two separate trials, but with markedly different emphases. The Guardian reports on Joshua Wong's sentencing to ten years under Hong Kong's national security law, framing the story almost entirely through a quote from Amnesty International, which argues the law has become a tool to punish dissent rather than genuinely protect national security - a critical but somewhat detached framing. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in a piece signed only with the initials "stah.", and the Los Angeles Times, in Kanis Leung's report, both focus instead on the first trial of its kind in Macao - the sentencing of former legislator Au Kam San to twelve and a half years. The sharpest departure comes from Ulf Poschardt's column in Die Welt, which casts Joshua Wong as a hero fighting Chinese authoritarianism and uses his case as a springboard to accuse the West of passivity and failing to back freedom fighters against China, Russia, Iran, Venezuela and Turkey - an explicitly polemical, ideologically charged framing that goes well beyond the more restrained factual reporting of the other outlets.
