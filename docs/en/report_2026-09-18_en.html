AI existential risk and global regulation debate

⌄

Coverage of AI as both an existential risk and a regulatory battleground splits along several competing lines. Der Spiegel devotes by far the most sustained attention to the topic and stakes out the most alarmed, critical position: across multiple pieces it argues that AI is slipping beyond its creators' control, that corporate calls for regulation amount to hollow "safety washing," and that both Washington under Trump and Beijing are chasing geopolitical advantage rather than genuine safety cooperation - a view reinforced through interviews with Daniel Kokotajlo, who calls for a verifiable pause and mutual oversight. The Guardian echoes this skeptical tone but adds its own political-economic angle: one piece frames Dario Amodei's call to "pace" AI development as arousing suspicion from both Trump allies (who see protectionism) and safety advocates (who see it as insufficient), while another proposes an ideological alternative - a "socialist" AI model - as a response to both superpowers shaping the technology to serve their own hegemony. The Daily Telegraph strikes a similarly dramatic, moral register, reporting King Charles's warning to tech executives about "existential dangers" requiring urgent international cooperation, alongside a distinctly different personal column warning that the greater threat is deliberate misuse by anti-human environmental extremists engineering bioweapons - an angle unique to that outlet. The Wall Street Journal covers the same royal speech but frames it as Britain's bid to position itself as a neutral broker between Washington and Beijing, while adding sharper analysis elsewhere: one piece argues the very meaning of "AI safety" diverges fundamentally between the US (preventing loss of control) and China (preserving Party control), while a polemical opinion piece warns that American tech CEOs' self-restraint may end up benefiting China. The Washington Post lays out the fullest spectrum of the domestic American debate: one article presents arguments for and against regulation side by side, one op-ed (Reid Hoffman) calls for a pragmatic middle path, another (Ignatius) demands urgent expert-led regulatory frameworks while criticizing political paralysis in Washington, and yet another inverts the whole logic, arguing the real danger isn't AI itself but falling behind China - a stance echoed by the Los Angeles Times, which describes the Xi-Trump summit as hobbled by mutual distrust that makes any substantive deal unlikely. The Süddeutsche Zeitung contributes a distinctive complementary perspective: unlike the West's existential anxiety, it reports that China isn't having this debate at all - its priority is making AI maximally useful under tight political control as fast as possible, not whether it is too dangerous. Finally, USA Today steps outside the security-and-regulation debate entirely, urging AI's new tech titans to emulate old industrialists by visibly reinvesting in public goods to stave off a populist-socialist backlash.

Canada-EU rapprochement amid Trump trade tensions

⌄

Most outlets agree on the core facts – Trump's tariff threats and annexation rhetoric toward Canada, and Carney's response of turning to the EU, including von der Leyen's proposal to grant Canada "associate member" status – but tone and emphasis diverge sharply. The Washington Post offers the most measured news-analysis framing, describing Canada "embracing" Europe in reaction to tariffs and annexation talk, quoting Carney defending sovereignty without strong editorializing. Die Welt and Süddeutsche Zeitung are more skeptical: both frame Carney's push as a rational strategic response to an unreliable United States, but stress that the alliance remains largely symbolic given Europe's internal divisions, sluggish response, and continued security dependence on Washington, with Süddeutsche Zeitung adding that von der Leyen's clumsy remark actually made matters worse. The Daily Telegraph takes the most cynical angle, portraying von der Leyen's offer as largely a symbolic gesture designed to provoke Trump and boost Carney's leverage, while highlighting internal EU tension over whether Brussels sees itself as a superpower or a leader of "middle powers." The Guardian is the most active and varied voice: alongside straightforward reporting on Carney's push for associate membership and the legal ambiguity of the proposed status - echoing the Telegraph's point that no legal definition exists - it also runs several sharply opinionated pieces directly attacking Trump, arguing his clash with Canada is nonsensical, that it reflects a chaotic, unstrategic imperialism comparable to his moves on Greenland and Venezuela, and that his ultimatum undermined the legal foundations of the North American trade agreement, framing Carney's defiance as part of a broader battle over the international order.

US-China AI rivalry and safety dialogue

⌄

Der Spiegel offers by far the most extensive coverage, running a whole series of articles and interviews - including with Daniel Kokotajlo - that converge on a consistent, alarmed stance: AI is slipping beyond the control of its creators, and geopolitical rivalry between Trump and Xi is blocking genuine regulation, whether from governments or from industry, whose self-policing efforts are repeatedly dismissed as hollow "safety washing." Der Spiegel also critiques the slow, fragmented regulatory response in Europe and Germany and warns of an escalating digital arms race that could end catastrophically. The Wall Street Journal frames the divide differently, arguing that Washington and Beijing do not even share a common definition of "safety" - for China the term protects the Communist Party's grip on power rather than humanity at large - and draws an explicit parallel to the Cold War nuclear arms race, urging procedures for mutual risk avoidance; alongside this, the paper also runs a more combative piece arguing that American tech CEOs' cautious self-restraint effectively benefits China. The Washington Post takes a domestically focused angle: one article insists the real danger is not AI itself but falling behind China, opposing any slowdown of US progress, while another piece from the same paper calls for a coherent, expert-driven US regulatory framework, contrasting American political paralysis with China's top-down control model. The Los Angeles Times offers a more measured diplomatic analysis, emphasizing deep mutual distrust that makes a meaningful agreement around Xi's Washington visit unlikely. The Guardian breaks from this US-versus-China framing entirely, criticizing both American and Chinese AI models as vehicles for their respective bids at global hegemony, and calls instead for a socialist, cooperative "third way" in AI development untethered from either rival technological empire.

AfD's rise in Germany and historical Nazi comparisons

⌄

Coverage of the AfD's rise spans nearly every outlet, but the framing diverges sharply. The Guardian casts the phenomenon in the starkest terms - describing it as an "earthquake" and existential threat to German democracy and Chancellor Merz's government with wider European ripple effects, while a companion piece stresses that the danger is national rather than merely eastern German, rooted in ideas of racism and superiority that postwar Germany was meant to have buried; a further piece even frames Jürgen Klopp's comments on football as a cultural counterweight to the AfD's gains. Süddeutsche Zeitung develops the historical angle most systematically, reviewing Philipp Ruch's book likening the AfD's rise to the NSDAP's sudden 1930 breakthrough as a disturbing "blueprint," and separately mounting an explicit defense of comparing the AfD to the Nazis as a legitimate analytical tool rather than an equation; the paper also dissects the party's aggressive streamer-driven media strategy and debates the constitutional mechanics of a possible party ban, arguing that federal coercion under Article 37 always comes too late compared to preemptive banning proceedings. Der Spiegel offers the widest range of critical analysis: one piece exposes how Alice Weidel is copying Trump-style, softened campaign tactics to mask extremism; another stages an internal debate between Spiegel journalists over whether the press should explicitly label the AfD far-right extremist or whether doing so feeds its victim narrative; a third brings in Swiss politician Gerhard Pfister, who rejects equating Switzerland's SVP with the AfD yet still endorses Germany's "firewall" against it; and a fourth channels an SPD argument that overemphasis on arms deliveries to Ukraine, at the expense of diplomacy, boosted AfD's electoral fortunes. Die Welt strikes the most pragmatic note, arguing after the Saxony-Anhalt election that neither a party ban nor blanket exclusion of the AfD will work, and calling instead for a substantive political offer addressing voters' concerns about migration and security - a stance that stands in clear contrast to the "firewall" approach favored elsewhere in the coverage.

German domestic politics: CDU/Merz leadership crisis

⌄

The German and Anglophone outlets each foreground a different facet of Merz's leadership crisis. So while Der Spiegel dwells on the internal power battle and The Guardian on the strategic failure against the far right, Süddeutsche Zeitung shifts blame away from the government and toward the AfD itself.

⌄
