Russia-Ukraine war and hybrid warfare against Germany/Europe

⌄

The German press supplies the dense core of this coverage, all converging on the suspected Russian drone plot against Leipzig airport as proof of a systematic hybrid-warfare campaign - sabotage, espionage, cyberattacks and propaganda - against Germany and Europe, though with distinct emphases. Der Spiegel is the most critical of the German government itself, accusing Berlin of hesitancy and of withholding evidence of Russian involvement for political reasons ahead of state elections, while pushing hardest for a robust response and greater European strategic sovereignty in security matters. Süddeutsche Zeitung offers the most granular policy coverage - suspect identification, sanctions details, statements from Kallas and Putin's denials - while also warning against domestic political polarization and a German debate on Russia detached from reality. Die Welt foregrounds the propaganda dimension, highlighting Russian rhetoric invoking Nazi-era "fascism" imagery against Germany, and calls for a more comprehensive European strategy including counter-propaganda. Among English-language outlets, The Guardian and The Wall Street Journal add a domestic political-polarization angle (notably the far-right AfD's rise) and skepticism about whether NATO's response is forceful enough, while the Daily Telegraph frames Putin's hybrid tactics as a deliberate doctrine rather than desperation, demanding stronger Western deterrence. The Economist is a notable outlier: it barely engages with the Leipzig incident itself, instead running a recurring set of analyses - several by former Polish foreign minister Radosław Sikorski - arguing Europe must stop fearing escalation and stop "protecting Russia from itself," and that a cornered Putin is driven to escalate by a personal, gangster-style survival logic rather than rational geopolitical calculation. USA Today keeps its focus narrower, on NATO's institutional readiness and defense spending, while Der Spiegel additionally tracks the intensifying air war over Ukraine itself as a related but distinct front of Russian escalation.

Iran-US conflict and Gulf escalation

⌄

Coverage of the escalating Iran-US conflict splits along clear lines of focus. The Daily Telegraph and The Guardian center on the same triggering event - a US strike that killed civilians at a wedding - with The Guardian emphasizing Iran's accusation of a "war crime" and the collapse of the truce, while The Daily Telegraph leans more into Iran's military retaliation and Trump's threats of even more devastating reprisals, up to obliterating the regime. Los Angeles Times and USA Today take a more analytical, distanced view, both portraying a war of attrition in which neither side can sustain the current cycle indefinitely - Iran ground down by sanctions and a naval blockade, the US strained by logistics and domestic politics - and both suggesting one side will eventually be forced to escalate to break the deadlock. The Wall Street Journal offers the most hawkish framing: one report presents the open-ended US military buildup as a deliberate strategy to crush Iran economically and militarily, quoting Trump's satisfaction with control of the Strait of Hormuz and Iran's collapsing economy, while a companion opinion piece goes further, explicitly urging Washington to press for regime change rather than settle for a face-saving deal. ⌄

⌄

⌄

Together, the coverage splits between The Economist's structural and regional diagnosis, the Wall Street Journal's geopolitical framing around China, and the Guardian's pointed indictment of collapsing US humanitarian commitment.

European sovereign debt crisis vs the US, France's fiscal woes

⌄

The bulk of coverage on this topic comes from The Economist, which runs multiple, closely related versions of the same core argument: although the United States carries the larger headline debt figure (having just passed $40 trillion), Europe has the more serious debt "problem" because its debt is fragmented across 27 states without collective backing, whereas the US benefits from reserve-currency status and stronger growth. The paper repeatedly singles out France as the destabilizing force - fiscal profligacy, populist politics ahead of the 2027 elections, and rising bond yields - warning this is dragging Germany down too and poisoning prospects for joint EU debt issuance, since no one wants to "run a joint tab" with a fiscally unreliable partner. Alongside this, The Economist also publishes more technical analyses of European bond markets, arguing that rising yields there stem from different causes than in America - chiefly a supply-side energy shock from the Iran war compounding worsening fiscal positions in France, Germany, and Italy - but are no less serious. The Wall Street Journal inverts this Europe-focused framing, instead casting America itself as the bigger source of global fiscal worry: it points out that the very countries at the center of the bond-market selloff are actually borrowing far less than the US, implying that American fiscal profligacy is the more distinct and severe problem. The paper also criticizes G-20 leaders, including Treasury Secretary Bessent, for tinkering with symptoms of rising yields rather than confronting the underlying causes of unsustainable debt and weak growth, while noting that the US at least benefits from AI-driven growth compared to the structural demographic decline facing Europe and Japan. The Guardian, by contrast, narrows the lens entirely to Britain, criticizing the government's Commons statement for lacking urgency about UK gilt yields at 5.2% and warning of a harsher market reckoning ahead. The result is a clear divergence in framing: The Economist trains its critical eye inward on Europe and France, The Wall Street Journal turns that scrutiny back onto America itself, and The Guardian confines the debate to the domestic British fiscal predicament.
