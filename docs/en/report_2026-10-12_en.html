Trump-Putin diesel deal and US-Ukraine rift

⌄

Nearly every outlet treats Trump's diesel deal with Putin and the accompanying "energy ceasefire" as a cynical move driven by domestic politics rather than genuine diplomacy, but each newspaper places the emphasis differently. The Daily Telegraph frames it as a story of betrayal: its news reporting documents Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy sites hours after the ceasefire was announced, while Con Coughlin argues in his column that Trump's greed will destroy his reputation, and Connor Stringer writes in his analysis that the whole affair is really about the US midterm elections rather than Ukraine and Russia; Tom Harris, in his column, urges Britain to fill the leadership vacuum Trump is leaving, and letter writers go so far as to call Trump himself "unfit to lead." The German-language press - Die Welt, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Süddeutsche Zeitung, Handelsblatt and Neue Zürcher Zeitung - follows a similar pattern: straight news coverage traces how Trump's demand that Zelensky step down and halt strikes on Russian refineries coincided with a deal that hands Putin fresh war financing, while commentators such as Nikolas Busse in Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung and Jens Münchrath in Handelsblatt warn in their columns that this amounts to a "betrayal" of transatlantic trust and call on Europe to act independently; Die Welt adds a distinctly domestic angle, noting that Germany's far-right AfD and the BSW actually praised the deal as a model for returning to cheap Russian energy. The American papers - Los Angeles Times, The Washington Post and USA Today - focus heavily on bipartisan backlash in Congress, highlighting a group of senators, including Republicans, who argue the deal violates US sanctions law and hands Putin a "financial lifeline." The Guardian brings a distinctly skeptical empirical angle through Simon Goodley, who cites experts saying the deal will barely affect US fuel prices given the tiny volumes involved, while Luke Harding stresses that Zelenskyy was not even informed of the ceasefire in advance. The Globe and Mail offers the sharpest foreign-government rebuke, with Prime Minister Carney publicly denouncing the deal as directly opposed to Ukrainian, Canadian and allied efforts to secure peace.

Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and regional escalation fears

⌄

Most outlets - The Daily Telegraph, Die Welt, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Handelsblatt, Globe and Mail, Los Angeles Times, USA Today, The Washington Post and Süddeutsche Zeitung - cover the story largely as straight news: the strike on King Khalid International Airport's terminal in Riyadh that killed around a dozen people and wounded over 300, followed by further attacks on other Saudi airports. Most share three common emphases: the threat to global oil supplies and shipping, the question of possible US military involvement under Trump, and fears the war could widen to draw in Iran and Pakistan. Die Welt and Handelsblatt both frame the attacks in nearly identical language as part of a "spiral of violence" driven by Iran's proxies, while The Washington Post stresses the exposed vulnerability of a key US ally and the Trump administration's reluctance to openly help fight the Houthis seven months into its war with Iran. Beyond the factual reporting, several pieces offer sharper argument: in a column for The Daily Telegraph, James Rothwell contends the Houthis have been persistently underestimated and now wield a sophisticated hypersonic "Palestine-2" missile, making Trump wary of all-out retaliation given depleted US missile stockpiles and memories of the Strait of Hormuz fallout after the Iran war. In a column for Süddeutsche Zeitung, Bernd Dörries analyzes the dilemma facing Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, for whom any retaliation risks further escalation, alongside Washington's parallel dilemma over intervention. Neue Zürcher Zeitung adds a broader regional angle: one piece, bylined by Rewer Thoffer and Daniel Böhm, examines Saudi Arabia's limited retaliatory options given hesitant allies like Pakistan and Turkey and lukewarm US appetite for further war, while a separate column by Daniel Böhm shifts focus away from Riyadh to argue that the fall of Taiz to Houthi forces could seal Yemen's fate and trigger a massive refugee crisis.

'China Shock 2.0' and European trade/auto defenses against China

⌄

Coverage splits between gloomy macroeconomic diagnosis and close reporting on the actual trade negotiations. In The Daily Telegraph, Roger Bootle warns in his column of a second, more severe "China Shock" driven by deliberate state policy rather than market forces, threatening manufacturing-heavy economies like Germany; Melissa Lawford extends this by arguing Germany is especially exposed after having grown complacent on dependence on Beijing. In Neue Zürcher Zeitung, Michael Froman similarly argues in his piece that China's subsidy-driven overcapacity model is unsustainable and risks a global crisis, calling for a coordinated realignment akin to the 1985 Plaza Accord. By contrast, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in reporting by Winand von Petersdorff, foregrounds the IMF's view that weak domestic consumption, not just currency manipulation, is the chief driver of China's surplus. The currency question also runs through Handelsblatt, where Martin Benninghoff's column and a separate staff report both describe Beijing flatly rejecting Western pressure - including Friedrich Merz's call for a Plaza-style dialogue - to revalue the yuan on Western terms. On the concrete EU-China hybrid-vehicle agreement, sources split between cautious welcome and sharp skepticism. Handelsblatt carries several competing voices: one report notes the EU trade commissioner and the German auto industry association welcoming the deal, while another unsigned piece criticizes the fact that its key terms remain secret - a critique sharpened in Jakob Hanke Vela's column, which argues the deal is vague and that China has broken similar promises before, so Europe should use the breathing room to cut dependency rather than relax. Süddeutsche Zeitung's Jan Diesteldorf likewise calls the Chinese concession a positive but fragile signal and urges Brussels to keep up pressure rather than ease off. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, meanwhile, reports in a more measured tone, framing the deal as merely a cautiously welcomed "first step." A further angle centers on the auto industry itself: The Guardian and the Globe and Mail both quote Volkswagen CEO Oliver Blume urging the EU to back domestic manufacturing through "Made in Europe" rules against Chinese competitors, in near-identical framing by Jasper Jolly and Rachel More respectively. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung's Niklas Záboji, reporting from the Paris Motor Show, describes Macron and Merz jointly demanding quotas and trade-protection instruments while still professing openness to "high-quality" Chinese investment. Another Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung piece, by Felix Schwarz, adds a consumer-facing angle, noting that the EU's removal of the de-minimis tariff exemption has already hit German revenues of Temu, Shein and Aliexpress. In the Los Angeles Times, David Hebert offers a parallel skepticism from the American side, dismissing the new U.S.-China "Trade Board" as an unenforceable, buzzword-laden framework.

Philippe Sands on international law and Germany's silence on Gaza

⌄
