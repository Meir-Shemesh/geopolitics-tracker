Trump's declining political and business support in the US

⌄

The three sources approach Trump's eroding support from distinct angles. In his column for The Daily Telegraph, Jeremy Warner focuses on the business world, arguing that mounting criticism from figures like Jamie Dimon signals a genuine loss of political momentum for Trump, one that could even open the door to a partial retreat from "America First" and a return to multilateral alliances with Europe. The Los Angeles Times, in a report by Jill Colvin and Linley Sanders, takes a public-opinion angle, showing that a large bipartisan majority of Americans blame Trump's tariff policy and his handling of the Iran war for persistently high prices, with his approval ratings on the economy and trade at record lows. In his column for The Washington Post, George F. Will offers a sharper partisan framing, arguing that most congressional Republicans deserve electoral punishment for enabling Trump's Iran war and trade war with China, while explicitly carving out two Senate candidates he believes should be spared. Where the Telegraph stresses defections among business elites and a possible strategic pivot, the Los Angeles Times grounds its case in economic polling data, and the Washington Post delivers an explicitly partisan electoral verdict ahead of the midterms.

Brazil's corruption scandal and 2026 election

⌄

Both sources agree that Brazil's upcoming election is unfolding under a cloud of corruption, but they foreground different stories. In The Daily Telegraph, Adrian Blomfield zeroes in on a sprawling banking fraud and bribery scandal centered on Daniel Vorcaro, portraying it as having corrupted Brazil's political, judicial and corporate elite across party lines and tainting both Lula's and Bolsonaro's camps alike; his vivid detail about women being forced to surrender their phones at a lavish party underscores the elite's moral decay. The Washington Post's Marina Dias, by contrast, takes a broader view of the national mood, arguing that fear rather than hope is what is driving Brazilian voters. She, too, notes corruption scandals swirling around both leading candidates, but layers in an additional dimension absent from the Telegraph's account: anxiety over possible U.S. interference under Trump favoring Flávio Bolsonaro, a risk she reports Brazilian intelligence has classified as "critical." Where the Telegraph's reporting dwells on the scandal itself as a cross-partisan rot, the Post situates that same corruption within a wider climate of dread that includes foreign meddling.

US federal debt and fiscal policy concerns

⌄

Both pieces are opinion columns warning about America's fiscal trajectory and agree that entitlement reform is essential, but they differ in emphasis.
