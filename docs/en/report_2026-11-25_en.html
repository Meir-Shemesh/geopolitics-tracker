Russia-Ukraine war: asymmetric operations, naval strikes, Baltic and Polish tensions

⌄

All four articles on this topic come from Die Welt, yet they offer notably different angles on the same underlying development. In his column, Jacques Schuster argues that Ukraine cannot defeat Russia outright, but that covert operations, drone strikes, and attacks on Russian naval vessels, including off Libya and in the Baltic, can inflict enough pain to push Putin toward negotiations, and he frames such strikes as legally and morally justified. A separate piece bylined by Alfred Hackensberger expands this picture, describing how Kyiv is extending its fight beyond Ukraine's borders to hit Russian "shadow fleet" and naval assets in Libya, the Black Sea, the Baltic and the Mediterranean, with the strategic goal of draining Russian oil exports and military logistics into Africa and the Middle East, a trend causing unease in NATO-adjacent states like Italy. By contrast, an unsigned news report from Die Welt conveys German Defense Minister Pistorius's warning against letting the war spread into the Baltic Sea following a suspected Ukrainian strike on a Russian freighter, even as he affirms Ukraine's right to hit military targets inside Russia and pledges continued German support. A fourth, straightforwardly factual report from Die Welt covers a brief air-alert in a Polish border district after a Russian drone entered Polish airspace, days after Russian cruise missiles had crashed on Polish soil, noting that Polish forces chose not to shoot the drone down. The overall contrast is between the more approving, almost strategic framing of asymmetric escalation offered by Schuster and Hackensberger, and the more cautious tone running through the Pistorius and Poland reports, which stress the danger of the war spilling beyond Ukraine's borders.

Dispute over carbon pricing and German climate policy

⌄

Coverage of this topic comes from a single source. In a column in Die Welt, Axel Bojanowski sharply criticizes four CDU parliamentarians who are demanding that Germany keep its national carbon price in place even after a planned EU-wide emissions trading system takes effect. He argues that such unilateral national measures do not help the climate but actually harm it, since they risk driving companies abroad (carbon leakage) without achieving real emissions cuts. For Bojanowski, the key rule for slowing global warming is reciprocity: only a coordinated effort by most states can meaningfully limit the climate problem, which is why he frames the CDU lawmakers' push for a national carbon price as Germany going down a mistaken "climate wrong turn." As this is the only piece addressing the issue, there is no cross-source debate to compare, only the columnist's own reasoned position against a faction within his own party's ranks.

Jensen Huang/Nvidia saga in US-China tech policy

⌄

All three articles on the Jensen Huang/Nvidia saga come from Die Welt, but they offer three distinct angles on the same episode, Huang's reinstatement as CEO after political pressure. In a joint column for Die Welt, Benedikt Fuest, Holger Zschäpitz and Nando Sommerfeldt frame Huang as an irreplaceable "general" in the global AI race, portraying Washington's intervention as a necessary move to safeguard American technological supremacy over China. In a separate column for Die Welt, Jan Klauth focuses on Trump's personal role, noting that Trump publicly admitted he had "talked to" and "convinced" Nvidia's board, and depicts a blurring of lines between the company's business interests and government policy, including concessions on export controls to China and political donations. By contrast, a third, unsigned, Die Welt report surfaces a far more serious dimension: a years-long relationship between Huang's partner, Alice, and an alleged Chinese intelligence officer, alongside claims that Trump knew of these findings before his administration cleared Huang and restored him to a key role shaping US-China chip policy. While the first two pieces frame the story as one of national strategic interest overriding corporate governance concerns, the third raises much sharper questions about espionage risk and political favoritism.

New German fuel price cap

⌄

This topic is covered by a single source. Die Welt, in an article by Daniel Zwick, reports on the German government's draft fuel price cap law (Kraftstoffhöchstpreisgesetz) set to take effect in January, laying out in detail the proposed pricing formula of 18, 15 and 20 cents depending on fuel type and region, along with its exemptions. The piece's emphasis is practical and cautionary rather than ideological: it foregrounds warnings from industry experts that the scheme could create regional winners and losers, distort pricing, and even risk supply shortages, framing the central question as whether security of supply can truly be guaranteed at all times, as one expert is quoted saying.
