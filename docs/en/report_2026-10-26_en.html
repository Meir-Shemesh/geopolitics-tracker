French nationwide riots and protests

⌄

Coverage of the nationwide French protests and riots - which drew over 400,000 participants on 17 and 25 October (unions claimed up to 800,000) and triggered mass looting, arrests and heavy policing - varies widely by outlet. Neue Zürcher Zeitung offers the broadest treatment: one piece by Jürg Meier simply tallies participation and arrest figures, another by Daniel Rickenbacher explores the mixed public reaction of fear and sympathy toward rioters, a reportage gives voice to young Parisian protesters frustrated over living costs and drawn to Mélenchon, and an interview with political scientist Jérôme Fourquet frames the unrest as symptomatic of France fragmenting into a disconnected social "archipelago" where respect for authority has collapsed. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in a piece by Antonia Krinninger, takes an atmospheric, human-interest approach, quoting a resident's remark that "it's civil war, but you get used to it" and dwelling on shopkeepers' fear and barricading. Die Welt, in a column by Til Biermann, instead highlights a subsequent protest where police successfully contained the chaos, framing it as a relative victory for law enforcement. The Daily Telegraph, in a piece by Vivian Song, frames the unrest primarily through its political payoff, arguing the chaos has boosted far-left leader Jean-Luc Mélenchon's presidential prospects. The Guardian, in reporting by Angelique Chrisafis, centers on civil-liberties concerns, describing how Macron's government is fast-tracking a "vandals pay" bill that unions and legal scholars warn exploits public anger to curtail the right to protest, while Le Pen's far right seeks to capitalize on law-and-order sentiment. The Observer, in a report by Kim Willsher and Hannah Roberts, situates the French unrest within a wider European wave that also includes Italy, emphasizing mass arrests and destruction; in a separate piece Kim Willsher profiles Saint-Denis mayor Bally Bagayoko, who calls for electing Mélenchon and founding a Sixth Republic to address systemic racism. Finally, The Washington Post takes a starkly different economic angle: in an editorial, it argues the unrest reflects unsustainable public spending that France's stagnant economy cannot support, contrasting it unfavorably with fiscal reforms in Denmark and Germany and calling for spending cuts rather than denial of fiscal reality.
