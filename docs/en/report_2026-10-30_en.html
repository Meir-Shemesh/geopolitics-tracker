Israeli election aftermath and coalition formation

⌄

All the outlets share the same basic facts - an inconclusive election, a record number of seats for Arab-led parties, and Likud's allegations of fraud and Qatari-Turkish interference - but they frame these very differently. The Daily Telegraph, in Henry Bodkin's report, presents the foreign-interference allegations largely at face value, as a serious official investigation. By contrast, The Washington Post, Los Angeles Times and The Guardian - in Julian Borger's reporting - frame Netanyahu's claims as an unsubstantiated attempt to smear Arab voters and delegitimize results that threaten his hold on power, quoting Eisenkot and other officials who flatly reject them. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung similarly reports the election commission's rejection of the fraud claims, while in a separate piece Christian Meier analyzes how the Zionist opposition is "self-blocking" by refusing to cooperate with Arab parties, and another unsigned piece in the paper provides a factual primer on the Arab parties themselves. Süddeutsche Zeitung, in Kristiana Ludwig's report, focuses on the formal investigation and the likely prolonged path to government formation, while Tomas Avenarius, in a column for Süddeutsche Zeitung, broadens the discussion to the deeper question of whether Arab-Palestinian citizens can meaningfully influence a state that defines itself as "Jewish and democratic." Die Welt, in Daniel-Dylan Böhmer's piece, sets Netanyahu's accusations directly against Eisenkot's counter-charge that this is a disinformation campaign meant to delegitimize the vote. The Economist stands out both for its sheer volume of coverage and for taking the clearest editorial stance of any source: across several pieces and a cartoon it argues Netanyahu has "neither won nor lost," that the opposition's refusal to work with Arab parties could be "its undoing," and that a fast-moving coalition including Arab parties is needed to remove him, while sharply criticizing his strategy of delegitimizing Arab political participation as calculated electioneering. Los Angeles Times additionally offers a personal angle with its profile of Gadi Eisenkot's rise as Netanyahu's leading rival and the prospect of a historic coalition with Arab-led parties.

Lula's election win and Bolsonaro family's flight from Brazil

⌄

Coverage of Lula's win splits largely along an economic-market axis versus a democratic-political one. The Economist returns repeatedly, across several pieces, to a single consistent argument: markets were right to be rattled by Lula's surprise victory, and he must urgently name a credible finance minister and curb spending to avert a fiscal crisis and recession - his win, in this recurring framing, is not a mandate to double down on populist policy. Die Welt, in Holger Zschäpitz's column, echoes this market-focused angle, describing how the result caught investors completely off guard and triggered a dramatic crash in Brazilian stocks and the currency, linking the shock to prediction markets ahead of the US midterms. The Guardian, by contrast, frames the story primarily as a victory for democracy: reporting by Tom Phillips and Tiago Rogero presents Lula's win as a blow to Trump's regional "orange wave" strategy and to right-wing allies across Latin America, quoting Lula's own description of it as a rebuff to US interference. In a Guardian column, Jordana Timerman tempers that enthusiasm, arguing the victory halted but did not reverse the region's rightward drift, while in a separate Guardian column Jonathan Watts draws broader lessons from the win for social and environmental movements worldwide. On the Bolsonaro family's flight, The Economist, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung and USA Today report fairly factually that Flávio Bolsonaro's self-declared claim to leadership from exile was rejected almost unanimously, including by his own party, while Neue Zürcher Zeitung takes a sharper critical angle: Alexander Busch's column argues the flight and Flávio's grandiose rhetoric are backfiring and strengthening Lula, and Simon Hehli's column states outright that Flávio "is unsuited to martyrdom" given the incriminating evidence against him. Süddeutsche Zeitung, in Jan Heidtmann's piece, adds a personal angle on Justice Alexandre de Moraes as the Bolsonaros' nemesis, noting that he himself is now under pressure from a corruption scandal. Der Spiegel, meanwhile, treats the event only briefly within a domestic German roundup, simply noting that the right-populist axis in Latin America is not growing for now.

Russia-Ukraine war and ceasefire diplomacy

The Guardian takes a more institutional, diplomacy-focused approach, factually reporting Zelenskyy's announcement of renewed trilateral talks, while Dan Sabbagh's report exposes how a Trump-Putin oil deal keeps Russian diesel flowing to the US even as critics call it a reward for Russia's war and Ukraine suffers deadly drone strikes. AI distillation debate and US-China AI competition

⌄

The Economist dominates coverage of this topic and pushes a consistent, contrarian line across multiple pieces: it argues Washington should not try to ban Chinese "distillation" of American AI models, since the practice cuts consumer prices, keeps China's progress tethered to American innovation rather than fostering genuine independence, and ultimately cements America's technological lead rather than eroding it. In a related strand, the magazine also takes on the AI industry's "let-it-rip" camp that opposes regulation altogether, arguing that aggregate risks - cyberattacks, agentic superintelligence, labour displacement - justify government action, and that sensible US regulation would slow China down rather than cede ground to it. This economically-minded, somewhat sanguine framing contrasts sharply with The Daily Telegraph, in Matthew Field's reporting, and The Guardian, which both cover the same story from a security and espionage angle: an alleged Chinese intelligence asset reportedly targeted Nvidia's Jensen Huang in an apparent effort to circumvent export controls on advanced AI chips, a framing that treats the US-China AI contest as a counter-espionage problem rather than an economic one. In his column for The Washington Post, David Ignatius strikes a more alarmed note, warning that the United States lacks a coherent AI safety strategy compared with what he portrays as China's more disciplined approach, and urging the Trump administration to use the upcoming Trump-Xi summit to build modest safety dialogue mechanisms before disaster strikes - a tone far less reassured than The Economist's. In his piece for Süddeutsche Zeitung, Max Muth brings a European angle, celebrating Mistral's new open-weight model as a strategic win that reduces Europe's dependence on both American and Chinese AI. In her column for Neue Zürcher Zeitung, Marie-Astrid Langer examines the issue through the lens of domestic US politics, reporting a growing backlash against AI and data centers that both parties are exploiting ahead of the midterms. Der Spiegel, finally, adds an entirely separate security dimension, reporting on how AI chatbots could ease the construction of biological weapons - a concern connected to the broader debate over unregulated AI rather than the US-China rivalry specifically.

Lagarde's early ECB departure and succession battle

⌄

Coverage of Christine Lagarde's early departure from the ECB presidency diverges sharply in emphasis. The Daily Telegraph and Die Welt both frame it as a political-strategic move: Lagarde is stepping down early to let EU leaders secure her successor before France's presidential election, amid fears that a Le Pen victory could threaten the central bank's independence. The Guardian, in Phillip Inman's report, offers a very different angle, quoting Lagarde herself as wanting the "freedom of speech" to engage with world affairs beyond monetary policy, tying the eurozone's struggles also to the Russia-Ukraine war and Trump's trade war. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung runs two takes: a straight analytical piece on the horse-trading between Germany, France, Spain and the Netherlands over the succession, alongside a column by Christian Siedenbiedel arguing sharply that the resignation is politically motivated - a Macron maneuver to shape the succession - and warning against choosing a "convenient" rather than competent, independent successor. Neue Zürcher Zeitung likewise carries two voices: Cornelius Welp describes the "poker game" between Dutch and Spanish contenders, while Michael Rasch, in his column, is far more critical, calling the timing of the departure "undignified" and stressing that Lagarde is leaving the ECB in "rough seas" amid France's crisis and persistent inflation partly fueled by the Iran war. Süddeutsche Zeitung shows an internal contrast too: Alexander Mühlauer, in his column, praises Lagarde's tenure as a "stroke of luck for Europe" and speculates her resignation is meant to save France from a Le Pen presidency, while a jointly bylined report by Claus Hulverscheidt and Markus Zydra sticks to a more measured factual account of the economic and political backdrop. The Economist, across several pieces, frames the story primarily through a structural economic lens, emphasizing France's fiscal crisis and soaring bond yields, arguing that Lagarde's successor faces the thankless task of persuading Paris to cut spending, and detailing the political tug-of-war between Germany's preference for a hawkish Dutch candidate and France's push for a French chief economist, with the ECB expected to withhold help until France fixes its own fiscal house.

Rising oil and gas prices amid the Iran war

⌄

Coverage of this topic splits between dry market analysis and more human or political framings. The Economist devotes four pieces to untangling what it calls the "oil-market riddle": despite severe disruption to Gulf exports from the war, prices have not spiked as expected, partly because China's ban on refined-product exports and falling crude imports offset the shock, even as rising freight costs through the Strait of Hormuz keep Brent above "fair value"; the paper nonetheless warns that a large US strike on Iran after the midterms could still push prices to $130-140 a barrel. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in Catherine Bosley's analysis, focuses on Europe's exposure, reporting that gas prices have broken through 100 Euro per MWh due to the Ukraine war, the Iran conflict and the Hormuz chokepoint, with depleted storage pointing to further rises - and inflationary pressure - over winter. Süddeutsche Zeitung draws a direct line from disrupted Gulf diesel output and Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries to the soaring profits of Shell, Total Energies and OMV, folding this into Germany's domestic political fight over a windfall profits tax, which Merz and Reiche oppose but the SPD backs. By contrast, The Daily Telegraph, in Jonathan Leake's report, and The Guardian, in Jillian Ambrose's report, both cover Shell's profit surge from the same conflict-driven price rises but foreground public backlash, quoting climate and anti-poverty campaigners who accuse energy firms of profiteering from war and climate disaster, with The Guardian's critics describing executives as "popping champagne on the deck of a sinking planet." The Globe and Mail, in Sopheng Cheang's report, offers a starkly different human angle, following hundreds of passengers stranded in Cambodia after a cruise ship suspended its round-the-world voyage when fuel costs tripled amid curtailed Hormuz traffic - spotlighting the direct toll on ordinary people rather than the macroeconomic picture other outlets emphasize.
