Trump-Xi summit and US-China relations

⌄

Nearly every source agrees the Trump-Xi summit was heavy on ceremony and light on substance, but they diverge on what that means. The Guardian, in pieces by Amy Hawkins and Mark Saunokonoko, coins the term "diplotainment" to argue the event produced symbolism rather than progress on trade, Taiwan, AI or human rights. Los Angeles Times reaches a similar verdict - Michael Wilner calls it a "managed stalemate" - while a companion analysis argues the pageantry itself was a propaganda win for Beijing, with Trump treating personal rapport as an end in itself compared to Xi's more instrumental approach. The Washington Post offers a parallel framing: Michael Birnbaum and Cate Cadell describe the warm reception as a sharp break from earlier confrontation that granted Xi legitimacy without clear American gains, while another Post piece cites critics warning the U.S. is "letting China into the house" and eroding its own leverage. Fareed Zakaria, writing his own column in the same paper, breaks from this critical consensus, rejecting Cold War analogies and calling instead for "managed interdependence" rather than full decoupling. German coverage is notably sharper: Die Welt's Gregor Schwung and Süddeutsche Zeitung's Lea Sahay (along with Peter Burghardt and Charlotte Walser) read Trump's warmth as a sign of American weakness, arguing Washington can no longer afford open confrontation, with Sahay pointedly noting Trump's silence on China's support for Russia and Iran. The Wall Street Journal, meanwhile, frames the core problem as an asymmetry in perception - Trump sees a personal friendship that can bend events, Xi sees a useful instrument - leaving Beijing's authoritarian model and Iran policy unaddressed. The Daily Telegraph adds a distinct angle on tech and Taiwan tensions playing out among the dinner's business guests, while Süddeutsche Zeitung's Reymer Klüver and the Washington Post's Gerrit De Vynck, writing separately on AI, both note the irony that Xi voiced public concern about artificial intelligence risks while Trump dismissed them, even as neither side pursues serious regulation.

Russia-Ukraine war: fighting, economy and home front

⌄

Coverage of the Russia-Ukraine war here spans a wide range of angles, with each outlet emphasizing a distinct facet of the conflict. The Guardian offers two perspectives: Charlotte Higgins documents Kyiv residents bracing for a brutal winter under Russian strikes on infrastructure, while Sam Wollaston's feature contrasts two British dinner guests debating whether the UK should stay out of the war (tied to pro-Brexit sovereignty arguments) or recognize that a war in Europe affects everyone. Iran: US pressure, Strait of Hormuz and escalation

⌄

Coverage of the US-Iran standoff splits sharply along institutional versus personal lines. The Wall Street Journal, unsigned and through reporters such as Benoit Faucon, frames the US Treasury's pressure campaign against Iran's trading partners - the UAE, Turkey, and Gulf states - in almost approving terms, quoting officials who bluntly declare "you either trade with Tehran or Washington" and noting that this economic squeeze has achieved more in weeks than earlier sanctions regimes managed in years. Alexander Ward and Summer Said, also in the Journal, report that Trump privately expects to resume bombing Iran after the midterms even as he rejects Iranian ceasefire proposals, treating the war's ambiguous status as leverage to ensure Iran never acquires a nuclear weapon. Paul Berger's Journal interview with DP World's CEO extends this economic lens, warning that the Strait of Hormuz closure has already damaged Gulf trade and could presage disruption to other vital global waterways. Los Angeles Times reporters Cara Anna and Aamer Madhani offer a more sympathetic reading of Tehran's position, describing Iran's conditional offer to reopen the strait and resume nuclear talks as a genuine, if contested, diplomatic overture, while highlighting internal Iranian divisions between moderates and hard-liners and Saudi doubts about its sincerity. By contrast, The Washington Post shifts the focus inward to American conduct: columnist Linda Gustitus condemns Trump's threat to "annihilate" Iran as a dangerous assertion of unchecked presidential power that a passive Republican Congress has failed to challenge, while Shiva Farivar, in a letter to the paper, writes as an Iranian American that such rhetoric disrespects the Iranian people and their heritage regardless of the regime's own failings. The Daily Telegraph rounds out the coverage with two distinct angles: Emily Smith reports Iranian President Pezeshkian's insistence that Supreme Leader Khamenei survives and that Tehran is open to de-escalation, while Amy Gibbons covers domestic British criticism accusing Foreign Secretary Miliband of handing Iran a propaganda victory by shaking hands with its foreign minister.

Migration and asylum policy in Europe

⌄

Coverage of migration policy splits along national and ideological lines. The Daily Telegraph carries the most extensive coverage, all from a critical domestic angle: in a letter, John Bauer recounts his village's fight against plans to house large numbers of male asylum seekers at RAF Barnham, arguing the scale is unsuitable for small rural communities; Brent Smith, also writing in a letter, contends there is no moral case for importing foreign labour while domestic workers sit idle, framing mass immigration as a threat to wages and even national security; and further letters complain about bureaucratic chaos caused by the EU's new Entry/Exit System, which left British travellers penalised by faulty exit records. Together these Daily Telegraph voices depict European migration and bureaucratic policy as burdening ordinary Britons and their communities. Die Welt, by contrast, in an article by Ricarda Breyton, takes an institutional European view, documenting how the EU's new joint asylum system, GEAS, is failing to deliver on its core promises of swift migrant registration and orderly transfers between member states, with Germany and Italy locked in ongoing disputes over responsibility - a systemic failure at the EU level rather than a local British grievance. The Guardian, in reporting by Ashifa Kassam, offers a third perspective entirely, portraying Giorgio Meloni's government in Italy as cynically courting the far right through a decree capping migrant children in classrooms and banning burqas and niqabs, framing it as a distraction from the government's lack of concrete achievements and citing criticism from the opposition and human rights groups.

The 'Islamic Left' and antisemitism in Western urban politics

⌄

Two opinion pieces in Die Welt paint the phenomenon in sweeping and alarmed terms. Alan Posener argues that across cities like New York, London and Berlin a coalition of progressive and Muslim voters is forming whose main emotional glue is antizionism, which too often tips over into violence against Jews, and he calls for decisively confronting this danger. Harald Martenstein, also writing in Die Welt, goes further in naming the phenomenon the "Islamlinke" (Islamic Left), describing it as a movement held together by shared rejection of the West and its values and a high degree of reality denial, and warns that its radical positions on policing, immigration and citizenship pose a serious threat to Berlin and Europe, calling its antisemitism and pro-crime agenda dangerous and irresponsible. By contrast, Christian Zaschke in Süddeutsche Zeitung narrows the focus to the specific German case of the Left Party (Die Linke), arguing that it must expel members and activists who deny Israel's right to exist, since such positions are antisemitic regardless of how they are framed as pro-Palestinian solidarity, and warning that failure to do so will hollow the party out from within. Where Die Welt constructs a broad, international picture of a dangerous urban coalition between the left and political Islam, Süddeutsche Zeitung's critique is narrower, directed at internal ideological discipline within one German party.

German domestic politics: extremism, AfD and democracy

⌄

Die Welt supplies the bulk of the analytical range on this topic, while Süddeutsche Zeitung contributes complementary, more targeted angles. In her column for Die Welt, Hannah Bethke argues that it is not the "firewall" against the AfD itself that is destabilizing German democracy, but rather the political establishment's defensive posture, which reviewing Anglo-American commentary on Chancellor Merz she says is inadvertently strengthening the party. Overall, Die Welt presents a spectrum of intellectual positions - from criticism of the establishment to legal caution and global demographic analysis - while Süddeutsche Zeitung emphasizes institutional stability alongside cultural critique and concrete worries about AfD identity politics.
