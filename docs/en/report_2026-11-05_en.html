US midterm election results and Republican rout

⌄

All the sources covering the 2026 US midterms - The Daily Telegraph, Die Welt, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, The Guardian, Neue Zürcher Zeitung, Süddeutsche Zeitung, USA Today and The Washington Post - agree this was a historic Republican rout, but diverge sharply in emphasis. Straight news reporting across Die Welt, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Neue Zürcher Zeitung, Süddeutsche Zeitung, The Guardian, USA Today and The Washington Post documents the bare facts: Democrats captured both chambers of Congress and governorships in red states like Texas, Georgia and North Carolina, with historic firsts among minority candidates. The Daily Telegraph, through Connor Stringer, adds a distinctive angle by reporting a public rift between Trump and Vice-President JD Vance, with Vance blaming the Iran war for the loss while Trump remains defiant. Several columnists offer competing interpretive frames: in Die Welt, Ulf Poschardt frames the defeat as democracy functioning properly through the peaceful alternation of power, while warning against a further leftward swing; Sandra Kegel in Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung argues Trumpism's reliance on permanent outrage ultimately exhausted voters; her colleague Frauke Steffens instead credits the Democrats' diverse "big tent" coalition; and Christian Weisflog in Neue Zürcher Zeitung cautions Democrats against over-reading the win as a mandate for a leftist agenda. At The Guardian, Rachel Leingang argues this amounts to a wholesale rebuke of the Maga movement rather than an ordinary midterm backlash, while David Smith frames Trump as the architect of his own destruction. At The Washington Post, Henry Olsen renders the Republican collapse in vivid geological terms as a "continent-wide rupture," while Theodore R. Johnson highlights the South's and Black voters' decisive role in delivering the "knockout blow." Exit-poll analyses, notably in Neue Zürcher Zeitung, single out the Iran war, fuel prices and the cost of living as the chief drivers, while Die Welt separately rounds up international press reactions describing the result as an "epochal disaster" for Trump.

Implications of midterm defeat for Trump's foreign policy

⌄

All the papers agree that the Republicans' brutal midterm defeat will not meaningfully constrain Trump's foreign policy - where his constitutional authority remains largely intact - but they differ sharply on whether the result will produce greater belligerence or forced restraint. At The Daily Telegraph, both John Bolton and David Blair, each in his own column, predict that a Democratic Congress will actually push Trump toward escalation: he will strike Iran again to prove his "relevance," squeeze Ukraine toward capitulation, and lean further into Putin and Xi. The Guardian, across several pieces, similarly paints a wounded president with nothing left to lose who may try to "finish the job" against Iran within months, while Patrick Wintour reports how Tehran is loudly, triumphantly claiming it engineered "regime change in America"; a separate Guardian analysis by Richard Partington instead stresses the economic fallout - inflation and oil shocks - framing the institutional checks-and-balances outcome as reassuring for markets. The Washington Post, through Mary Ilyushina and Gerry Shih, foregrounds foreign capitals' calculations that a weakened Trump could turn into an "angry duck" rather than a lame one, while Karen Tumulty treats the results more as a Democratic roadmap than a foreign-policy flashpoint. German coverage is notably more sober: Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, through Nikolas Busse and Andreas Ross, argues Trump's worldview won't change and he will keep pushing a Putin deal at Ukraine's expense, even as his grip on his own party loosens. Süddeutsche Zeitung, via Daniel Brössler and Hubert Wetzel, allows for modestly expanded room for Berlin and Europe to assert themselves but warns against expecting Trump himself to shift course. Die Welt offers the widest internal range: Gregor Schwung details Congress's new oversight tools, Jamie Dettmer warns transatlantic relations could actually worsen, and Katja Mitic relays Wolfgang Ischinger's caution against European "Schadenfreude." Neue Zürcher Zeitung takes an analytically cautious tone, stressing Trump retains broad unilateral power over tariffs and Iran and could even intensify both, while Anne Allmeling describes Iran trying to claim the result as its own victory against wary Gulf Arab silence. The Globe and Mail, by contrast, leans hardest into celebratory interpretation: Lawrence Martin, Andrew Coyne and Konrad Yakabuski, each in his own column, hail the outcome as a restoration of democratic sanity and checks on executive power after the Iran war and Trump's tilt toward Russia, while Paul Waldie compares his fall to populist collapses in Hungary and Serbia, and Jill Lawless quotes world leaders cautiously doubting any quick change in US policy.

Financial market reaction to the Democratic midterm sweep

⌄

All the outlets agree on the basic facts - stocks surged, oil fell, the dollar weakened - but they diverge in what they emphasize as the cause and significance. The Globe and Mail devotes the most extensive coverage, running several distinct factual reports: one framing the rally around hopes that political gridlock will curb both runaway spending and escalation of the Iran war, another tracking how investors repriced AI data-centre, clean-energy, defence and prison-operator stocks on expectations of tighter regulatory scrutiny, and a separate piece specifically on the drop in Elon Musk's Tesla and SpaceX shares amid prospects of congressional investigations into his political spending. The Washington Post, authored by Federica Cocco and Todd C. Frankel, similarly highlights the oil-price drop as a market bet that a Democratic Congress will make it harder for Trump to escalate the conflict with Iran. Among the German-language press, Christian Siedenbiedel in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung frames the story in sharply personal terms - declaring Trump himself "the day's election loser" - while also weighing both the chance of a calming Iran deal and the risk of a renewed strike. In his column in the Süddeutsche Zeitung, Gunnar Herrmann paints a similar picture but leans more heavily into the tension between market relief and the looming danger of a fresh attack on Iran, which he likens to a sword of Damocles hanging over the market. By contrast, the Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in a piece jointly bylined by five writers, takes a more forward-looking, analytical approach, surveying seven predictions for how united Democratic control of Congress will usher in a prolonged period of regulatory uncertainty for American business, particularly around AI, trade policy, and shifting corporate political donations. Joanna Partridge, writing in the Guardian, centers her account on the dollar's slide to a one-month low, while noting that even with Trump's agenda constrained domestically, he retains considerable sway over markets through trade, tariffs and foreign policy.

Russia's exclusion from international ice hockey over Ukraine war

⌄

Coverage of Russia's exclusion from international hockey splits between a proud, assertive Canadian framing and a broader European narrative of a growing boycott coalition. The Globe and Mail offers the most nationally triumphant angle: in two columns, Cathal Kelly argues that Canada is the first Western ally to take real action against Russia in sport rather than merely voicing concern, leveraging its dominance in hockey to stand up to the Court of Arbitration for Sport and the international hockey federation. Alongside this, a Canadian Press report in the same paper takes a more factual approach, detailing how Canada used its immigration powers to bar Russian and Belarusian athletes from the world junior championship despite a CAS ruling favouring their reinstatement, and documenting the backlash, including sponsor threats and criticism from Russia's ambassador. The Guardian provides a wider, pan-European view, listing a series of national federations - Canada, Finland, Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Latvia and Czechia - refusing to face Russian teams after the CAS ruling, emphasizing player-safety concerns alongside the ongoing war in Ukraine. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in a piece by Nicola Berger, expands on this same trend and adds a political-propaganda angle, arguing that ice hockey serves as an important propaganda tool for Putin's power apparatus and describing the growing "anti-Russia alliance," including visa refusals and further boycotts. German-language coverage also features a sharper normative critique: in a column in Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Christoph Becker attacks the International Olympic Committee and international sports bodies for allowing Russia back into competition despite the war, arguing this betrays sport's values and that Russian teams should not be allowed to compete, especially in Germany. Süddeutsche Zeitung, in a report by Johannes Schnitzler, focuses on the mounting pressure on the German federation and government to take a clear stance ahead of the 2027 World Championship in Germany, amid boycott threats from federations and sponsors. Thus, while The Globe and Mail celebrates Canada's unique leverage, the other sources focus more on the collective European dimension and on the moral and political questions facing the future of international sport.

⌄
