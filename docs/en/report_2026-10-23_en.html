France: nationwide riots and political crisis

⌄

Coverage of the nationwide French riots and political crisis splits along several distinct lines. The Daily Telegraph leads with tourism fallout, with Vivian Song reporting on foreign holidaymakers cancelling trips amid roughly 550,000 protesters and over 2,510 arrests, while a separate Telegraph piece by Emma Taggart pivots to the financial consequences, showing how unrest and budget deadlock pushed French borrowing costs above those of the United States. That market angle is echoed by The Globe and Mail's Stefano Rebaudo, who notes France's risk premium hitting its highest level since the 2011 eurozone debt crisis, and by two pieces in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung - an unsigned analysis citing market strategists on surging risk premiums, and a column by Niklas Záboji arguing that markets are right to price in rising country risk even though France is "not yet a second Greece." Alongside the economic framing, The Guardian, through Jon Henley and a further unsigned report, focuses on the legal and judicial fallout: Macron's banning of "illegitimate" protests and lawyers' sharp criticism that fast-track courts are being used as a political tool against social anger. The Los Angeles Times, in a joint report by Samuel Petrequin and Sylvie Corbet, offers a more procedural account of the tug-of-war between courts and government as a Paris ban is overturned even as violence and looting continue elsewhere. The Washington Post's Ellen Francis adds an international dimension, detailing Macron's rebuke of Trump and other foreign leaders for falsely framing the unrest as migration-driven rather than rooted in cost-of-living grievances. A critical thread running through Neue Zürcher Zeitung, via Daniel Steinvorth, and Der Spiegel portrays Macron as absent from the crisis, touring Gulf states for oil and gas deals while France burns, with Der Spiegel adopting a particularly sardonic tone. Die Welt contributes two further angles: one report tracks Marine Le Pen extending her poll lead by capitalizing on the unrest, while another, by Leonhard Landes, describes Germany's Left party leader citing France as a model for German protests. Süddeutsche Zeitung's Roland Preuß examines similar fears in Germany about imported violence more skeptically. Finally, the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung also publishes an interview with historian Hélène Miard-Delacroix, who frames the unrest as a symptom of a "fin de règne" and collapsing political trust, drawing comparisons between far-right movements in France and Germany.

US-Israel military posture and threats toward Iran

⌄

Coverage of US and Israeli preparations for a new round of strikes on Iran diverges sharply by source. The Daily Telegraph, in Alexandra Snow's report, most closely echoes the operational logic of the planners, describing joint US-Israeli fighter drills near Iran's border and quoting officials who believe "one more heavy attack" could collapse Tehran's economy and force concessions. The Washington Post centers on Netanyahu personally, presenting his explicit call to overthrow the Iranian regime "once and for all" against a backdrop of American military buildup, while noting Washington's own caution that it will not strike before the midterms and Iran's stated preference for diplomacy. The Guardian, in Patrick Wintour's piece, instead foregrounds Iran's threatened response, quoting security officials who vow a geographically and temporally expanded war - striking oil facilities, cables and datacentres across the Gulf - framing the story around the risk of uncontrolled escalation rather than Western military planning. The Los Angeles Times, across two reports including one by Melanie Lidman, keeps closer to the maritime and economic fallout - tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz - while adding a distinctly Israeli domestic-politics angle, arguing that Netanyahu's push to attack Iran is "not even a question" and that an upcoming close election may determine whether the strike actually happens. Süddeutsche Zeitung offers a more analytical, balanced framing, suggesting both sides believe they can outlast the other even though the standoff is unsustainable for each, while reporting plans for renewed bombardment after the US midterms alongside Revolutionary Guard tanker attacks. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung adds a sharply critical domestic Israeli layer absent elsewhere, reporting not only the hardening rhetoric toward Iran but also opposition fears that Netanyahu could use a new war as a pretext to postpone the Knesset election. Die Welt stands apart in tone: in an interview conducted by Carolina Drüten, former Mossad intelligence chief Zohar Palti does not focus on imminent strike planning but instead argues the West is making a strategic mistake on Iran, calls for coordinated Western economic pressure, praises Germany's expanding security and intelligence role - including the Arrow 3 system - and cautions that Israel cannot sustain endless wars on six fronts, a notably more restrained voice than the operationally focused reporting elsewhere.

Russia-Ukraine peace talks and skepticism over Putin's intentions

⌄

Coverage of the renewed US-Russia diplomatic contacts converges on deep skepticism toward Putin's sincerity, but sources diverge in focus. The Washington Post, in reporting by Mary Ilyushina and colleagues, argues that gestures like the prisoner release and agreement to resume trilateral talks are largely cosmetic and benefit Russia most, while a separate Washington Post piece surveys Ukrainian and Russian experts who see the talks as a stalling tactic rather than genuine engagement. The Guardian, in a report by Luke Harding and Pjotr Sauer, foregrounds the cynical timing of events, noting Russia launched a massive ballistic missile and drone attack on Kyiv just hours after agreeing to resume talks, quoting Zelenskyy's accusation that both the weapon choice and the targeting were deliberate attempts to maximize civilian harm. Die Welt offers the most extensive treatment: in a column, Filipp Piatov relays Czech President Petr Pavel's warning against direct US-Russia talks that exclude Europe and his description of Putin as "misinformed and paranoid," while in a separate interview Pavel argues the war could drag on indefinitely or escalate without a change in strategy. Another Die Welt piece, by Florian Sädler and Gregor Schwung, dissects Putin's seemingly conciliatory statement to envoy Witkoff as driven by tactical and imperial calculation rather than genuine desire for peace, pointing to a major Russian strike on Kyiv that followed within hours. Wolfgang Ischinger, in both an interview and a signed piece for Die Welt, warns that a Trump-Putin diesel deal sends a dangerous signal by normalizing business with the Kremlin despite continued attacks, invoking the "Nord Stream mistake" as a cautionary parallel. By contrast, an opinion piece in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung relays the Swedish newspaper Dagens Nyheter's argument that Trump's shift on Ukraine is not a reversal but merely a more open admission of his longstanding wish for a Russian victory, while a separate Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung report cites The Wall Street Journal's criticism of Putin's hostage release as a cynical power play. A dissenting voice appears in a reader's letter to the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung by Volker Gerdesmeier, who breaks from all other sources by arguing Zelenskyy, not Putin, is the one avoiding peace for personal enrichment, and praises Trump's disengagement. Meanwhile, The Globe and Mail and Los Angeles Times, both via reporting by Derek Gatopoulos, along with the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, provide more straightforward factual accounts of heavy Russian missile strikes on Kyiv coinciding with the diplomatic overtures, quoting Ukrainian officials insisting that only strength, not appeasement, will end the war.
