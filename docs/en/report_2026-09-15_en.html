AI arms race: US-China competition and existential risk

⌄

Coverage splits along several distinct axes. The Wall Street Journal offers the most sharply nationalist-competitive framing: multiple pieces there insist the real danger is China rather than AI itself, that regulation could hand Beijing a decisive advantage, and that America's failing education system - not industrial policy - is the true handicap; the paper also reports relatively sympathetically on Trump's dismissal of existential-risk warnings as a Chinese "hoax," alongside a separate piece documenting growing internal alarm among AI researchers themselves about a reckless race. Los Angeles Times covers the same Trump stance but frames it more critically, as a political analysis contrasting his dismissiveness with bipartisan and industry calls for regulation. The Daily Telegraph takes a cautious, moralizing tone, likening AI to nuclear weapons and arguing that being first offers no lasting advantage, so safety should trump speed - a softer position than the Journal's zero-sum framing. The Guardian mounts the most radical critique of the industry itself, attacking AI companies' "exceptionalism" and their "betrayal" of the living world, and portraying industry-proposed regulation as an attempt at regulatory capture. Süddeutsche Zeitung takes a related but more targeted critical line, arguing that corporate warnings about "runaway" AI distract from real failures in cybersecurity and from AI's quiet erosion of jobs, while in a separate piece the same paper, together with Die Welt, warns of dangerous European dependency on American and Chinese technology and urges urgent investment in infrastructure and supply chains. The Washington Post instead focuses on engineering solutions to the alignment problem and on already-materializing harms - such as AI-guided autonomous weapons in the Ukraine war - treating the danger as tractable through better design, in contrast to The Guardian's more fatalistic tone and the Journal's nationalist framing.

Israeli 'Naza' Gaza documentary and citizenship-stripping threat

⌄

All four outlets - The Daily Telegraph, The Guardian, Die Welt and Süddeutsche Zeitung - cover the uproar over the "Naza" documentary and the Israeli government's threat to strip its directors' citizenship, but differ in emphasis and tone. ⌄

Houthi threat, Red Sea shipping and energy security

⌄

Coverage splits along national lines into two distinct axes: British and American outlets frame the Houthi threat as a strategic-security crisis, while the German press treats the same underlying events mainly as a driver of domestic fuel prices. The Daily Telegraph takes the most overtly political stance, with one piece urging the British government to formally proscribe the Houthis as a terrorist organisation to curb rising energy bills, arguing past leniency only let the group grow stronger, while another warns that the Red Sea shipping route is once again imperiled by an Iran-backed Houthi offensive and criticizes Trump for failing to back Saudi Arabia against the escalation. The Wall Street Journal offers a similarly hawkish but less overtly political analysis, warning that the Houthis - emboldened by the wider war with Iran - have seized a critical maritime chokepoint, compounding the blow from a drone strike on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, together amounting to an underappreciated and growing threat to global energy security. By contrast, Die Welt and Süddeutsche Zeitung address the same geopolitical backdrop - the Strait of Hormuz, Houthi attacks, the Ukraine war - not as a security threat but as the explanation for surging German fuel prices, engaging in a domestic policy debate: Die Welt argues the real culprit is squeezed refinery margins rather than crude prices or taxes, chiding German politicians for chasing price caps and windfall taxes while missing this point, and casts Trump's push to limit Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries as economically rather than geopolitically motivated. Süddeutsche Zeitung, across its two pieces, takes a clear market-liberal line, rejecting price caps or windfall taxes as responses to scarcity and attributing the Gulf and Red Sea escalation largely to Trump-driven disruption, insisting the only real answer is reduced consumption rather than artificially masking prices - a stance implicitly at odds with the British and American outlets' push for tougher military and political action against the Houthis and Iran.

Climate change economics and policy debate

⌄

The three outlets approach climate change from markedly different angles. Die Welt takes the most skeptical stance, challenging the economic foundations of climate policy itself: it argues that influential cost studies like the Stern Report and RCP8.5 scenarios have been politically driven and tend to overstate climate damage, citing economists who question whether uncertain, exaggerated projections justify sacrificing economic growth. The Guardian takes essentially the opposite position, warning that an unprecedented "hyper" El Niño threatens severe economic, agricultural and infrastructural harm, and sharply criticizing governments for expanding fossil fuel extraction instead of confronting the climate crisis head-on. The Los Angeles Times, meanwhile, focuses on a more local and practical dimension, examining how local governments in California have failed to meaningfully address climate-driven coastal damage and suggesting the state may need to step in and force action, much as it did on affordable housing, while criticizing wealthy property owners who block effective solutions. Together, the coverage spans a spectrum: Die Welt questions whether aggressive climate policy is economically justified at all, The Guardian urges governments to act more urgently against an looming crisis, and the Los Angeles Times zeroes in on the failure of implementation at the local level.
