Russia's hybrid warfare and sabotage threats against Europe

⌄

Coverage of this topic is dominated by The Economist, which runs a cluster of pieces - several sharing near-identical headlines - all arguing that Russia's "grey-zone" campaign of sabotage, cyberattacks and drone incursions against Europe is intensifying. Their shared thrust is a call to action: Europe's response has been too slow and fragmented, and it must move from passive defense to active deterrence and retaliatory measures such as sanctions and asset seizures, since, as one piece puts it, "what Moscow respects most is strength." A related Economist piece sharpens the focus on NATO specifically, arguing the campaign is designed to test Article 5 resolve and make the alliance appear powerless. The Daily Telegraph, by Nick Squires and James Badcock, offers a more concrete angle, reporting on alleged Russian plans for seaborne drone attacks on Italy, Spain and France meant to exploit political divisions and disrupt elections, stressing that "no one is safe" anymore. Die Welt contributes a specific strategic-geographic angle: in an opinion piece drawing on ISW analysis, Leander F. Badura warns that Russia could probe a limited hybrid operation in Narva to fracture NATO cohesion without risking full-scale war, arguing Putin will avoid direct attack only as long as NATO's response might jeopardize his goals in Ukraine. Süddeutsche Zeitung presents two distinct angles: in a signed piece, Sina-Maria Schweikle asks whether Russia is expanding the war beyond Ukraine given mounting hybrid attacks on NATO states and calls for containing Russia, while a separate unsigned article highlights the paradox of the United States, under Rubio, still inviting Putin to a G20 meeting in Miami even amid this escalation, framing it as pragmatic diplomacy. Der Spiegel adds a distinctly technical angle, describing annual Norwegian exercises to protect satellite-navigation systems from jamming and citing research showing Russia has possessed space-based jammers since 2019 and regularly tests them over Europe - providing concrete evidence for the broader threat other sources describe more generally.

US-China trade relations and market access

⌄

The Economist devotes five separate pieces to this topic, all converging on a single skeptical thesis: Donald Trump's claims that China is "opening up" to American firms are contradicted by business reality. Across its coverage, the magazine argues that record chip sales mask persistent restrictions, that American corporate sentiment remains negative despite improved diplomatic rhetoric because tariffs, tech-export curbs, rare earths and Iran remain unresolved flashpoints, that even expanded market access would mean little since key sectors stay dominated by state-favored Chinese firms and obstructive local authorities, and that American businesses are actually retrenching in China as returns fall - with semiconductors the lone bright spot, itself now endangered by the tech rivalry and Beijing's drive for self-sufficiency.
