AI safety regulation debate

⌄

Coverage of AI safety regulation splits fairly sharply along ideological lines, with Los Angeles Times and The Washington Post pushing for stronger oversight while The Wall Street Journal leans skeptical, and The Guardian framing the issue through a British/international lens. Los Angeles Times mounts the most forceful advocacy: several pieces attack Trump's hands-off stance as dangerous and out of step with public opinion, argue that the "AI race" is really about profit and power rather than intelligence, and call for a global treaty modeled on nuclear arms control, while also reporting approvingly on Governor Newsom's reversal toward tougher California rules including a mandated "kill switch." The Washington Post, via Fareed Zakaria, takes a more analytical middle path, rejecting the simplistic "race ahead vs. pause" framing and arguing the real danger lies in unchecked machine autonomy - calling for mandatory testing and human oversight rather than reacting to AI's raw capability, while insisting such regulation wouldn't hand China an advantage. The Wall Street Journal, by contrast, offers a range of voices that collectively lean against heavy-handed restriction: one piece argues a verifiable US-China AI safety pact is essentially impossible and thus more risk than safeguard, a letter warns against overconfidence in doom predictions, Peggy Noonan's column urges taking AI leaders' own expressed fears seriously, and another column argues AI firms should be disciplined by public shareholders through IPOs rather than ideological founders, dismissing much of the "doom" hysteria while flagging China's unregulated open-weight models as the real geopolitical risk. The Guardian frames the debate internationally, reporting that the UK government is scrambling and that meaningful regulation likely requires US-China coordination it cannot achieve alone, while its letters page airs a more skeptical view - that hype about existential "superintelligence" risk is a deliberate distraction cultivated by AI companies to dodge accountability for present-day harms, alongside debate over whether AI could develop autonomous goals that render kill switches meaningless.
