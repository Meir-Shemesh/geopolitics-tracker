9/11 legacy, war on terror, and world order

⌄

Coverage of the 9/11 quarter-century mark diverges sharply by newspaper. The Guardian frames the anniversary around Osama bin Laden's own strategic failure, arguing his original goals - bankrupting America, ending Western support for Arab rulers, sparking a global Islamist uprising - never materialized, leaving al-Qaida and Islamic State as diminished, leaderless fringe movements. The Daily Telegraph takes a distinctly British military-strategic angle, contending the West grew complacent after the Cold War, mishandled the Afghanistan withdrawal, and hollowed out its armed forces, so must now rebuild military mass and strengthen alliances. Süddeutsche Zeitung offers the widest spread of angles: a straight chronicle of the attacks and their legal and geopolitical fallout, a piece blaming America's failure to invest in countering Muslim radicalization and demanding tech regulation, an essay tracing a direct line from post-9/11 fear and eroded rule-of-law norms to Trump's disdain for democratic institutions, and Timothy Snyder's more radical argument that the American response actually delivered what the attackers wanted, entrenched a militarized oligarchic economy, and even enabled Putin's rise and the war on Ukraine. USA Today keeps its focus domestic and human-scale: the persistent liberty-versus-security tension over surveillance law, how 9/11 conspiracy theories seeded today's culture of election denial and QAnon, criticism of war-glorifying video games and recruitment media, and a personal tribute honoring first responders and service members' families. The Wall Street Journal offers a more mixed, even contrasting picture: one piece credits the U.S. response with successfully deterring further attacks without sacrificing civil liberties, another argues it was precisely that response - not the attacks themselves - that reshaped the world order by draining U.S. resources and letting China and Russia rise unchecked, while a third traces how the initial national unity after 9/11 curdled into the partisan division and populism that later fueled the MAGA movement. The Economist, in three versions of the same piece, consistently argues that the sprawling post-9/11 legal and surveillance architecture has outlived the terrorist threat it was built for and is now being cynically repurposed by Trump to expand presidential power against migrants, cartels, and domestic political opponents like Antifa - framing this as a missed opportunity for reform. Taken together, the coverage ranges from cautious claims of success (Wall Street Journal) to arguments that the American response effectively defeated its own purpose (Süddeutsche Zeitung, Guardian), and from an emphasis on military and alliance rebuilding (Daily Telegraph) to warnings about domestic democratic erosion (Economist, USA Today, Süddeutsche Zeitung).

⌄

Coverage of the AfD's landslide in Saxony-Anhalt splits along fairly distinct lines between Der Spiegel, The Guardian, and The Economist. Relatedly, The Economist argues the AfD is now "making the weather" in German politics, forcing the CDU-SPD coalition into reactive water-downs of its agenda and leaving mainstream parties few options beyond sticking together. The Wall Street Journal adds an international angle, examining JD Vance's hope that the AfD's rise signals a conservative renewal in Germany, but concluding that the party's success is more likely to produce political instability and paralysis than effective governance, since it has yet to prove it can actually govern.
