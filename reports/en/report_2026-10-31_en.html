Closure of Novaya Gazeta and press repression in Russia

⌄

All five sources treat the closure of Novaya Gazeta as a landmark moment for press freedom in Russia, but they differ in emphasis and texture. The Daily Telegraph, in Emily Smith's report, stresses the symbolic finality of the moment, quoting the image of "turning off the lights" in the newspaper's home of thirty years, and frames the closure as a direct act by Putin ending independent journalism in Russia altogether. The Guardian, in Shaun Walker's report, quotes the paper's editors extending the meaning beyond the institution itself, presenting the closure as emblematic of the systematic crushing of Russian society as a whole, not merely of one outlet. The Los Angeles Times takes a more institutional, fact-based angle, attributing the closure directly to pressure from Russian authorities and security services and linking it explicitly to the intensified crackdown on dissent following the invasion of Ukraine. The German-language press offers the most detailed and historically grounded accounts: Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in Friedrich Schmidt's report, and Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in Inna Hartwich's report, both foreground the chilling quote "they killed us" and trace the newspaper's decades-long history of murdered journalists, legal harassment, and loss of premises and funding as the culmination of sustained repression, giving the story more historical depth than the shorter, more symbolic Anglo-American framings.

Protest cultures and anti-riot liability laws in France/Europe

⌄

Seven articles cover this topic from three distinct angles: factual reporting on the French "casseurs-payeurs" liability law, political analysis of the backlash it provokes, and broader reflection on French protest culture versus Germany's. The Globe and Mail and Los Angeles Times, both bylined by Thomas Adamson, offer near-identical factual accounts of the French Senate's passage of a bill making rioters' parents financially liable for damages, noting the spread of similar crackdown measures to Spain, Italy and Britain, and both quote critics warning that seizing such payments would hit the poorest hardest. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in Christine Longin's article, dwells more on the domestic political fight over the bill, highlighting criticism from left-wing parties and rights groups who call it unjust collective punishment that could even hold mere bystanders liable and chill freedom of assembly. Süddeutsche Zeitung provides the fullest and most critical coverage, across three separate pieces: in his column, Oliver Meiler details the unprecedented security buildup ahead of a third nationwide protest day - mass arrests, harsh sentencing, and controversial AI surveillance - describing the government's approach as pure "le tout-sécuritaire" repression amid fears unrest could stretch into the 2027 presidential race; in another piece he portrays the state's failure to secure Paris on October 17, when looting and arson engulfed the area around Place de la République, framing it as a capitulation by Macron's government, which is now rerouting planned protests away from the city center. In a separate column, Nils Minkmar situates the unrest in a longer historical arc, arguing that French political culture has been structured around confrontation and revolt since 1789 through the Gilets Jaunes to the 2026 unrest, in contrast to a culture of compromise - though he also notes early signs of cross-camp cooperation emerging.
