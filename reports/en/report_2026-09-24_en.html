Trump-Xi summit and AI rivalry

⌄

Coverage of the Trump-Xi summit splits sharply between geopolitical skepticism, cautious optimism, and grassroots unease. In the Guardian, David Smith frames the meeting as unlikely to produce real breakthroughs, stressing that trade disputes, Taiwan and export controls will persist even alongside token cooperation on AI safety. Die Welt's Gregor Schwung goes further, arguing the visit is largely symbolic theater: Xi, he contends, still regards the US as a strategic adversary and is using Trump's professed "friendship" merely to buy China time to strengthen its position. USA Today's Robert Weiner and Emmbrooke Flather strike a starkly more optimistic note, portraying the summit as a major opportunity and offering concrete proposals - AI cooperation, rare-earth deals, curbs on tech espionage, and calculated ambiguity on Taiwan - to build mutual trust. The Washington Post's Ian Duncan, Riley Beggin and Cat Zakrzewski complicate that optimism from the industry's own vantage point, reporting that while AI executives hope for a US-China safety deal, deep mutual distrust and internal divisions within the Trump administration make any grand bargain improbable. Süddeutsche Zeitung's Hubert Wetzel takes the widest geopolitical view, arguing that AI has succeeded nuclear weapons as the decisive technology of global power, and that while Xi's China grasps and is positioning itself for this new order, Trump and the US fail to understand it - leaving Europe sidelined entirely. Finally, two Los Angeles Times letter writers inject a more skeptical civic perspective absent elsewhere: Steve Leon questions what exactly would be gained or lost by either side "winning" the AI race, while Jack Debes argues that reining in AI's existential risks requires halting new power plants and data centers - a concern about AI's material infrastructure that none of the geopolitical analyses address.
