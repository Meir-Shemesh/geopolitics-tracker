⌄

Coverage of the Hanning affair splits sharply between a narrow, personality-driven angle at The Daily Telegraph and the sprawling, multi-pronged investigation mounted by Der Spiegel. In her report, Emily Smith for The Daily Telegraph frames the story around a single striking detail - Hanning's appeal to Chancellor Olaf Scholz for financial help amid the collapse of a Latvian bank he was tied to - presenting the affair largely as a tale of personal corruption and a dramatic fall from grace. Der Spiegel, by contrast, treats the case as a systemic crisis for German intelligence, publishing a string of news reports, an investigative feature, and interviews that each stress a different dimension. A separate investigative piece traces how Hanning monetised his post-retirement network and insider knowledge for dubious clients, including Russian oligarchs, authoritarian Azerbaijan and Chinese military contacts, arguing he knowingly damaged his own legacy. Another Spiegel report widens the lens institutionally, asking whether the scandal should derail BND president Martin Jäger's push to grant the agency offensive cyber and sabotage powers against Russia, given the lack of stronger oversight. In two interviews, security expert Peter R. Neumann calls the leak a "worst-case scenario" that risks eroding trust with allied services, while also arguing Germany is engaged in a "fully hybrid confrontation" with Russia and that the reform should proceed alongside stricter disclosure rules for former intelligence officials taking outside consultancy work. Where The Daily Telegraph tells a contained story of individual wrongdoing, Der Spiegel constructs a far broader picture of institutional crisis, international distrust and the stakes for Germany's intelligence reform.

History and future of nuclear arms control

⌄

All three articles on this topic come from Der Spiegel, and together they form less a clash of competing views than a layered historical narrative built up in stages. The first piece surveys historical peace research showing how the United States and the Soviet Union once came close to agreeing to abolish their nuclear arsenals entirely, framing it as a near-miss opportunity. The second, "Wunder von Reykjavík", zooms in on that very moment - the 1986 Reagan-Gorbachev summit - quoting physicist and peace researcher Götz Neuneck's verdict that "the golden age of arms control is over," while also stressing that arms control is more important than ever today given the collapse of New START, the growing number of nuclear-armed states, and China's nuclear buildup. The third article, a column in Der Spiegel by Olaf Stampf, carries the story further forward, tracing the history of missile defense from Reagan's SDI through Putin's new weapons systems to Trump's "Golden Dome," and arguing that mutual distrust has revived a Cold War-style arms race. Stampf adds a dimension the other pieces do not address directly: the need to fold China into future disarmament talks, which he describes as turning arms control into an almost unsolvable "three-body problem." Together, the three pieces move from the near-realized hope of the 1980s, to a diagnosis of the old order's collapse, to an analysis of a harder, more multipolar challenge ahead.
