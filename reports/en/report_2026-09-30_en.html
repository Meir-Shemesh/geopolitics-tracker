Russia-Ukraine war: fighting, strikes and economic toll

⌄

Coverage of the war splits into four distinct angles. The Guardian focuses on the economic toll, reporting Ukraine's economy minister's warning that missile alerts cost $45 million an hour in halted business activity, while also flagging corruption concerns eroding trust among Western donors. The Los Angeles Times, by contrast, frames Russia's intensifying missile and drone barrages on Kyiv as a deliberate escalation designed to break Ukrainian resolve even as Kyiv offers to negotiate, emphasizing the military strain caused by dwindling Western air-defense supplies. In a column for Süddeutsche Zeitung, Sergey Maidukov, who fled Donetsk in 2014, draws on personal experience to argue that Russia's signature method of warfare is to render places uninhabitable before occupying them, warning that Putin's terror tactics, now reaching Kyiv, are meant to extract concessions Ukraine would never grant under direct military pressure. Die Welt takes a more technologically optimistic tack: in his column, Christoph B. Schiltz portrays Ukraine's multi-phase "Operation Vivaldi" as a breakthrough in modern warfare combining drones, ground robots and infantry, with major implications for NATO's defense planning - though he notes experts caution it is not yet a decisive turning point.

AI risk, regulation and industry self-interest

⌄

Six opinion pieces across four newspapers debate who really benefits from the AI industry's loud warnings about the dangers it itself creates. In separate pieces for Süddeutsche Zeitung, Gunnar Herrmann and Max Muth, and Jannis Brühl, both argue that apocalyptic warnings from executives like Sam Altman and Dario Amodei function as a strategic play for "regulatory capture" - pushing tough rules that would entrench incumbents and squeeze out smaller rivals, effectively assembling a cartel; Brühl calls on states to impose binding liability rules rather than let companies dictate the terms themselves. Writing in USA Today, Dace Potas reaches a similar conclusion from an opposite, market-skeptical-of-regulation angle: he sees Big AI's calls for oversight as a cynical bid to block competitors, and warns that burdening US firms with regulation would simply hand China the advantage in the global AI race. By contrast, Senator Josh Hawley, in a Washington Post op-ed, sets aside the question of regulatory motive and instead demands that AI companies face full legal liability and criminal penalties for the harms their systems cause, rejecting any "free pass." Writing in Die Welt, Benedikt Fuest takes a markedly different angle, describing a real and accelerating loss of control by AI developers themselves over their own systems, with concrete security and geopolitical fallout including state-sponsored hacking from Russia, China and Iran, and warning that the EU and US regulatory response merely offloads the problem onto bureaucracies ill-equipped to handle it. Economist Markus Brunnermeier, interviewed in Süddeutsche Zeitung, adds a further concrete concern - AI agents' potential to manipulate financial markets in ways humans can no longer track - and urges urgent international regulatory coordination, aligning with the pro-intervention camp but out of substantive alarm rather than suspicion of corporate motives.

Chinese imports threat to UK/EU industry and tariffs

⌄

Both articles address the risks Chinese imports pose to European industry, but they pull in opposite directions. Writing in The Daily Telegraph, Hans van Leeuwen cites Chatham House research to warn that without new trade protections, cut-price Chinese manufacturing could "wipe out" Britain's and Europe's industrial base within five years, framing the UK's current openness as risky compared with the EU's move toward protective tariffs. Daniel Wetzel, in an opinion piece for Die Welt, takes essentially the opposite view of a concrete protective measure: he argues that the EU's new tariffs on Chinese electrical steel (GOES) used in transformers actually endanger Germany's energy transition, since they make critical grid components more expensive and scarcer at a time when Europe lacks sufficient domestic production capacity. The contrast is thus between The Daily Telegraph implicitly urging more protection against Chinese competition, and Wetzel warning that protectionist tariffs themselves can backfire when applied to essential inputs Europe cannot yet produce on its own.
