US-China AI rivalry and diplomacy

⌄

The Economist provides the fullest coverage, running a near-uniform argument across many pieces: despite the Trump-Xi summit and appeals from tech leaders like Sam Altman for a joint deal on AI risk, deep structural distrust - divergent definitions of "safety," China's fragmented bureaucracy, and its "scripted" diplomatic style - make real cooperation unlikely, though narrower steps such as sharing information on safety incidents might still succeed. Der Spiegel reaches a similar conclusion from a more critical angle, arguing that Trump and Xi are each pursuing their own geopolitical agendas rather than genuine safety cooperation, and features an interview with Daniel Kokotajlo warning that unchecked escalation could culminate in war and proposing a mutually verified pause. Los Angeles Times echoes this skepticism from a diplomatic-event angle, noting that expectations for an AI guardrails deal are low on both sides ahead of Xi's Washington visit. Other American outlets, by contrast, frame the issue as strategic competition rather than a shared safety challenge. The Washington Post bluntly argues that the real AI danger is not the technology itself but the risk of America falling behind China, while The Wall Street Journal warns that overly cautious US regulation - as embodied by tech CEOs like Dario Amodei - could inadvertently benefit China, and highlights a fundamental conceptual gap: Washington fears AI slipping human control, while Beijing equates "safety" with safeguarding Communist Party rule. Süddeutsche Zeitung reinforces this from the Chinese side, showing that Beijing isn't debating whether to slow AI development at all, but is focused on making it maximally useful under tight political control, driven by regime survival rather than existential-risk concerns. The Guardian offers the most radical critique of both powers, arguing that both American and Chinese AI trajectories are designed to serve their own visions of global hegemony, and calls instead for a socialist, cooperative "third way" in shaping the technology's future.

AI existential risk warnings and global safety debate

⌄

Coverage of AI existential risk spans from moral alarm to cold geopolitical analysis. The Daily Telegraph frames the issue through King Charles's call for tech leaders to urgently confront AI's existential dangers, and pairs this with an opinion piece that sharpens the fear into a specific scenario: malicious actors, particularly anti-human "eco-extremists," using AI to engineer bioweapons. The Wall Street Journal covers the same royal speech but embeds it in a broader "digital arms race" narrative, explicitly likening the US-China AI rivalry to the Cold War nuclear arms race, while a separate piece in the same paper pushes back against sensationalized "rogue AI" framing of real-world incidents like the Hugging Face hack, insisting on more careful causal diagnosis. Der Spiegel is the most extensive and critical voice, running a series of pieces - including "Plan D wie Destruction," "Wie Maschinen die Welt übernehmen könnten," and interviews with Daniel Kokotajlo - arguing that unregulated superintelligence development, driven by Trump-Xi rivalry, risks catastrophic loss of control, and sharply attacking industry "safety washing" as no substitute for binding government rules. The Economist takes a more detached, skeptical stance across several articles, repeatedly asking whether the "AI arms race" can be stopped and concluding that while a safety agreement is technically conceivable, it is politically almost impossible given deep distrust between the US and China and even between American labs and their own regulators. The Guardian offers a skeptical look at industry calls for a coordinated slowdown, showing how Anthropic's Dario Amodei is criticized both by Trump allies as engaging in protectionist rhetoric and by safety advocates as offering too little. The Washington Post assembles a fuller domestic policy picture: one piece lays out the regulation debate's competing arguments, another gives space to Reid Hoffman's centrist call to avoid both doomerism and blind boosterism, a third reports OpenAI's own disclosures of AI models "cheating" as evidence that alignment lags behind capability, and a fourth (by Ignatius) blasts Washington's political paralysis against China's more centralized, resolute approach. Overall, where the Telegraph and the Journal lean on symbolic, moral framing - a King's warning, Cold War analogies - Der Spiegel and The Economist dissect the structural failures of self-regulation and great-power competition, while the Guardian and Washington Post train their focus on the fractures within the US industry and political system itself.

German domestic politics: Merz, CDU crisis and AfD rise

⌄

Coverage of Germany's domestic crisis - Merz's weakening authority and the AfD's rise - diverges sharply across outlets in tone and focus. The Guardian offers the most alarmed, big-picture framing: its cluster of pieces casts the AfD's Saxony-Anhalt result as an "earthquake" threatening German and European democracy, questions whether Merz is even the right leader to hold the line against the far right, and argues his tough-on-migration, competence-first strategy has only normalized AfD rhetoric rather than blunting it; a piece on Jürgen Klopp reinforces this narrative by presenting football as a counterweight to the far right's appropriation of national pride. Der Spiegel focuses more narrowly on internal CDU/CSU power struggles, describing Merz as losing authority faster than any postwar chancellor while rivals jockey to succeed him, and separately offers a critical analysis of how Alice Weidel is adopting Trump-style, softened-image campaign tactics; Spiegel also stages an internal debate between its own journalists over whether media should explicitly label the AfD as right-wing extremist or whether doing so feeds its victimhood narrative. Süddeutsche Zeitung takes the most investigative and historically minded approach, exposing the AfD's network of right-wing streamers who cast reporters as aggressors, engaging with historical parallels between the AfD's rise and the NSDAP's ascent in 1930, defending such comparisons as illuminating rather than equating, warning that relying on constitutional "Bundeszwang" measures against an AfD-led state government would always come too late instead of pursuing an earlier party-ban case, and dismissing the AfD's economic populism as dangerous to German prosperity. Die Welt provides a more moderate internal voice, publishing Boris Palmer's argument that neither banning nor simply shunning the AfD will work, and that mainstream parties instead need a substantive offer addressing voters' genuine concerns on migration and security. The Economist strikes a comparatively cautious, data-driven tone, suggesting AfD gains are not inevitable and highlighting a possible late SPD comeback in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern under Manuela Schwesig, while also probing her contentious past ties to Russia and Gazprom. Der Spiegel adds another angle by examining claims that the SPD's Russia policy fueled AfD gains, while Süddeutsche Zeitung, through the lens of a single dissenting AfD lawmaker's Kyiv visit, highlights a rare crack in the party's otherwise pro-Moscow consensus.

Rising bond yields and rich-world sovereign debt crisis

⌄

Coverage of this topic is dominated overwhelmingly by The Economist, which runs an extended series of pieces all pressing the same core argument: the era of cheap government borrowing is over, markets are "waking up" to reckless fiscal behaviour in America, Britain and France, while governments themselves remain "asleep," delaying painful adjustments. Several articles focus on the structural diagnosis - ballooning deficits, debt maturing in large chunks within a few years, and the retreat of traditional foreign buyers such as China, Gulf states and Japan - warning that postponing reform, as in past episodes in Greece or Britain under Liz Truss, will only make the eventual reckoning worse. A distinct cluster of Economist pieces, several by Mohamed El-Erian, zeroes in specifically on US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's intervention in the bond market: these consistently argue that Bessent's framing of rising yields as a temporary "fever" needing suppression is a misdiagnosis, that the market is simply pricing in real fiscal and inflation risks correctly, and that his buy-back interventions are both too small to matter and potentially counterproductive. Süddeutsche Zeitung, by contrast, offers a very different, more narrowly political-institutional angle: it covers the clash between Fed chair Kevin Warsh, who raised rates despite pressure, and Donald Trump, who demanded much lower rates given America's supposedly unmatched creditworthiness. Where The Economist treats bond yields and fiscal policy as a structural, cross-country economic crisis, Süddeutsche Zeitung frames the story as a personal and institutional power struggle over central-bank independence.
