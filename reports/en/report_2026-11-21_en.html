EU-UK post-Brexit relations

⌄

Coverage of the EU-UK summit shares a common factual core across most outlets, Die Welt, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Süddeutsche Zeitung, Neue Zürcher Zeitung, Los Angeles Times and The Guardian's straight news reporting all note the deals on easing food trade, linking emissions trading systems, and launching a youth mobility scheme, alongside the unresolved dispute over student tuition fees. Beyond that shared baseline, emphasis diverges sharply. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung's reporting by Thomas Gutschker and Johannes Leithäuser stresses the haggling and persistent "lack of trust" between Brussels and London even after a decade, while a separate commentary in Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung takes a firmer editorial line, arguing Brexit was a mistake that weakened Britain, the EU, and Europe as a whole, though full UK re-entry is not currently advisable. The Guardian devotes by far the most space and range of angles: triumphant reporting quoting PM Andy Burnham declaring "the deal is done," an analysis by Peter Walker framing Burnham's push as a calculated gamble resting on shifted public opinion, reporting by Rowena Mason on constituency-level polling showing most seats, including many that voted leave, would now back rejoining, a satirical sketch by John Crace mocking Nigel Farage's grievance politics, and economic commentary (including by Richard Partington) noting Brexit has cost UK GDP per head 6-8%, while other Guardian pieces caution that the deal is not an unqualified win, since Britain must pay in and align with EU rules without a vote, echoing Switzerland's position. The Washington Post, in unsigned reporting, foregrounds the broader geopolitical backdrop, a souring economy and a world order destabilized by Trump and Putin, as what has made "Brexit regret" newly acceptable, and frames Burnham as deliberately using incremental deals to reopen the Brexit question itself. Neue Zürcher Zeitung's Antonio Fumagalli highlights Burnham's celebratory tone alongside the conditions Brussels attached and continuing domestic political division, while Süddeutsche Zeitung's Martin Wittmann characterizes the step as a "big leap" for Britain but a "small step" for EU members, judging full re-entry politically unlikely before 2029.

Russia-Ukraine war: strikes, negotiations and Poland missile incidents

⌄

Coverage of the September missile incident in Poland is broadly convergent but differs in framing and depth. Die Welt, Neue Zürcher Zeitung (in Paul Flückiger's report), Los Angeles Times, The Guardian (in Jakub Krupa's report) and The Washington Post all report straightforwardly that Russian missiles and a drone landed on Polish territory during a NATO exercise, and that Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk called it a deliberate "provocation," prompting NATO jet scrambles and allied statements of solidarity. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung covers the same event across two separate pieces, one quoting Tusk's claim that the impacts were deliberately timed to coincide with "Coalition of the Willing" troop exercises, and another situating the incursion within a much larger Russian missile and drone assault that killed at least eight people and damaged bridges in Kyiv, giving Polish calls for reinforced air policing a broader wartime context that the other outlets's spot reports do not emphasize as strongly. On the diplomatic track, Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung offers additional layers: in a column for the paper, Nikolas Busse warns against being fooled by any appearance of diplomatic momentum from the Shenzhen summit, arguing there is no real evidence Putin wants to negotiate and that Europe must act in coordinated fashion rather than fall for Russian divide-and-conquer tactics, implicitly criticizing German Defense Minister Pistorius's recent conduct. A separate Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung report covers Chancellor Merz framing German support for Ukraine's reconstruction as a future economic "dividend," while yet another piece in the same paper documents Pistorius's apology to a US official after calling his criticism "pretty stupid," underscoring transatlantic friction over Ukraine's long-range strikes into Russia. A fourth Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung article, by Matthias Wyssuwa and Michaela Wiegel, surveys behind-the-scenes negotiation efforts toward a possible settlement, detailing Merz's conditions, French skepticism, and a shifting US stance on security guarantees. Neue Zürcher Zeitung also runs a column by Andreas Rüesch dismissing Russia's demand for NATO withdrawal from Eastern Europe as a historically baseless narrative masking imperialist ambitions, criticizing Western "Putin understanders." Two further pieces, by Volker Pabst in Neue Zürcher Zeitung and Michael Neudecker in Süddeutsche Zeitung, shift from the battlefield and diplomacy toward technology, describing Ukraine's drone-relay networks built to reduce dependence on Elon Musk's Starlink and its defense-tech startup scene operating under an "innovate or die" ethos.

Shenzhen trilateral summit: Trump, Xi and Putin

⌄

Coverage of the Shenzhen trilateral summit converges on a shared skepticism that it produced any real substance, but the sources diverge sharply in emphasis and tone. Several Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung commentators frame it as pure stagecraft: Jochen Stahnke argues in his column that Beijing's "summit diplomacy always follows a script aimed at maximum symbolism with minimal results," while a satirical column in the same paper mocks the nuclear-briefcase imagery and China's condescension toward a diminished Russia; the paper also carries translated Dutch, Italian and Spanish commentary arguing Europe emerged humiliated and sidelined. Die Welt offers two distinct angles: Ibrahim Naber argues in his column that both the Berlin and Shenzhen summits left only illusions, with no "Yalta 2.0" division of the world and no real shift in Russia's position, while Daniel-Dylan Böhmer writes in his column that apparent great-power agreement on Iran conceals dangerous unresolved power struggles. Süddeutsche Zeitung frames the story through a German-European lens: Daniel Brössler writes in his column that Chancellor Merz staged the Berlin gathering as a deliberate counter-image to Shenzhen, Nicolas Richter argues in his column that Europe has shown welcome unity while condemning Trump's fawning over Putin, and Gerhard Matzig uses the summit's table arrangement in his column as a satirical lens showing China staging itself as the true power behind the new order. The Washington Post, by contrast, leans into institutional reporting: Michael Birnbaum reports that the three leaders agreed to jointly weaken international oversight bodies like the UN and ICC, with further Post pieces documenting the administration's lack of transparency and warning that foreign leaders could exploit Trump's appetite for flattery. The Guardian adds sharp analytical framing: David Smith writes in his piece that a domestically wounded Trump sought validation from autocrats abroad, a companion piece calls the summit a propaganda win for Putin and a diplomatic win for Xi, and a further analysis reads the circular table's seating arrangement as a visual signal that Xi held the dominant position over both Trump and Putin. Los Angeles Times, including Michael Wilner's reporting, stresses the absence of concrete outcomes, describing a pattern of "summitry without substance" even as the war in Ukraine intensifies and China continues circumventing U.S. export controls. Neue Zürcher Zeitung closes by arguing that despite the staged display of tripartite dominance, the real distribution of global power is far messier than the summit's choreography suggests.

ICJ genocide ruling against Myanmar over the Rohingya

⌄

Coverage of the International Court of Justice's finding that Myanmar's military committed genocide against the Rohingya splits between straightforward factual reporting and pointed interpretive framing. USA Today, Los Angeles Times (by David Rising and Grant Peck), and The Guardian (by Jason Burke) largely report the ruling itself, Myanmar's rejection of it as "unfair" or a "great disappointment," and calls from Rohingya advocacy groups for compliance. The Guardian adds a distinct diplomatic angle, detailing how the EU and UK hailed the ruling as a "landmark" while the US response was cautious and China questioned the court's legitimacy. The Washington Post, in an editorial, departs sharply from the other outlets by arguing that, despite the atrocities, the US should pragmatically re-engage with Myanmar's junta to counter Chinese influence and secure critical minerals, insisting that skepticism of the ICJ process should not be equated with denying the underlying genocide. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in a column by Meret Baumann, takes yet another distinct angle, linking the Myanmar ruling to the parallel genocide case against Israel over Gaza, arguing that the court's logic that a legitimate counterterrorism fight does not preclude genocidal intent could make a future finding against Israel more plausible, while noting important factual differences between the two cases. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung barely engages with the substance of the ruling, instead focusing on Malaysia's decision, coordinated with Turkey and the UNHCR, to resettle 10,000 Rohingya refugees in Turkey, noting only in passing that this came a day after the ICJ ruling.

European youth protests

⌄

Coverage of the November wave of youth protests across Europe splits clearly between straightforward reporting and deeper interpretive analysis, with Neue Zürcher Zeitung devoting by far the richest and most extensive treatment. The Guardian, in a piece by Jon Henley, and USA Today, in a report by Stephane Nitschke and Giselda Vagnoni, both offer broad factual accounts of tens of thousands of protesters across more than twenty countries, framing the demonstrations as a coordinated international movement against the cost of living, housing costs and militarisation, quoting slogans like "Fight for our future, not their wars". Neue Zürcher Zeitung, by contrast, builds a far more layered picture: one article examines how Italian unions and opposition parties are trying to co-opt youth-led strikes against the Meloni government for their own political ends, another covers Portugal's housing crisis through the eyes of an activist at a protest camp in Porto who describes police violence, and a separate piece explores how Donald Trump has become a hate figure on European protest posters, symbolizing young people's anger at the perceived abandonment of EU values. Sociologist Donatella della Porta, interviewed across several Neue Zürcher Zeitung pieces, provides the recurring theoretical framework, arguing that protests from Tirana to Lisbon are driven by shared anxiety about the future (war, climate change, artificial intelligence, conscription) and deep institutional frustration, and warning that ignoring these demands risks radicalizing young people; in another interview she argues European police have shifted from de-escalation toward a more "colonial", repressive style of policing. A young Albanian activist, Klevis Tako, interviewed by della Porta, adds a local voice describing anger at corrupt political elites in the Balkans. In Germany, Süddeutsche Zeitung, in a report by Roland Preuß and Kathrin Wiesel-Lancé, covers the "Gen Z regelt" protests as a domestic story centered on education cuts and conscription, while Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung runs two interpretive columns: Patrick Bernau argues that young Germans' anger reflects deep structural shifts (the end of a "golden decade", war, demographic aging) that no single reform can fix, whereas Ralf Euler warns that online "engagement" and viral outrage are no substitute for the unglamorous work of institutional participation, and that violence at protests undermines the legitimacy of the underlying demands.

German/European support for Ukraine

⌄

Five articles, from Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Die Welt and Süddeutsche Zeitung, expose different layers of the German debate over supporting Ukraine, from public opinion to official policy to personal cost. Die Welt frames the topic through a YouGov poll showing that nearly half of Germans want to scale back or halt aid, breaking the data down by party, age and East-West lines to highlight eroding public backing. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in one article, instead presents the government's official stance: Chancellor Merz praises deepening German-Ukrainian economic ties but makes further financial aid conditional on anti-corruption and rule-of-law reforms, while warning Kyiv against export fees or restrictions on intellectual-property transfer, a picture of calculated, conditional support rather than unqualified enthusiasm. In a separate piece in the same newspaper, Anna Sophie Kühne argues that Europe's fragmented development of parallel satellite systems, Germany's SATCOMBw4 and the EU's IRIS², is wasteful and strategically dangerous, especially given doubts about Elon Musk's reliability as the Starlink provider underpinning Ukraine's connectivity, adding a technological dimension to the question of Europe's dependence on outside support. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung also carries an interview in which Roland Siller contends that Germany's development bank DEG, financed mostly through its own resources rather than taxpayer money, is stepping in to fill the gap left by retreating donors such as USAID and China, including financing Ukraine's wartime economy, offering a quieter, long-term economic-support angle. By contrast, in a Süddeutsche Zeitung interview conducted by Detlef Esslinger, former SPD Bundestag member Michael Roth gives a personal and emotional account: his vocal public advocacy for arms deliveries to Ukraine since the war began exposed him to threats and a toxic climate of outrage in German politics, driving him into therapy, a stark contrast to Die Welt's dry polling figures and Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung's institutional calculations.

German domestic politics: AfD, SPD and the political center

⌄

Coverage of the AfD, the SPD and the state of Germany's political center splits mainly between Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung's wide, multi-angle alarm and Süddeutsche Zeitung's more measured, institutionally reassuring take, with Die Welt contributing a separate critique of the SPD's leadership. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung runs a cluster of pieces that together paint a picture of mounting concern: Torsten Hoffmann exposes how AfD strategist Hans-Thomas Tillschneider openly invokes Heidegger's 1933 Nazi-era rectorial address to reshape university policy, Jasper von Altenbockum's column traces an internal AfD rift over the ethnic-nationalist concept of "remigration" that threatens to split the party, and Theresa Weiß and Reinhard Bingener report on apparent coordination between the AfD and BSW in Saxony-Anhalt to seize control of the parliamentary oversight committee for domestic intelligence.
