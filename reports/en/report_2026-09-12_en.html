Houthi threat to Red Sea shipping and global oil supply

⌄

Three papers cover the Houthi seizure of Yemen's Red Sea coast and the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint, but each frames it differently. The Daily Telegraph offers the most dramatic and politically charged angle: one piece portrays the move as a calculated, Iran-linked bid to "strangle" global oil supply, dismissing Houthi assurances of safe passage as untrustworthy, while a companion piece turns the story into a domestic British complaint, attacking the government for fixating on issues like bus fares while "the world burns" and demanding urgent investment in defence and foreign policy. Süddeutsche Zeitung stays closer to a regional strategic analysis, with its two pieces emphasizing how the Houthi capture of a key island and strikes on Saudi oil infrastructure serve both the rebels' own goals and Iran's, stressing potential fallout for world trade and the timing relative to US midterm elections. The Washington Post widens the lens to a broader global energy crisis, linking the Bab al-Mandab attacks to simultaneous turmoil in the Strait of Hormuz and quoting experts warning of a compounding "constriction" of the energy flows sustaining the world economy, with particular attention to the leverage this gives the Houthis over the United States and Saudi Arabia. Thus, while the British coverage pairs geopolitical alarm with domestic political criticism, the German coverage focuses on regional strategic interests, and the American coverage frames the story as part of a two-front global energy crisis.

Rise of Germany's AfD and societal reaction

⌄

Coverage of the AfD's rise, especially its election victory in Saxony-Anhalt, reveals sharply different emphases across the sources. The Daily Telegraph zeroes in on one dramatic, concrete angle: the party's call to use German-made weapons for "remigration" and mass deportations, focusing on the scandal and political fallout of that specific statement rather than offering deeper structural analysis. The Guardian, by contrast, situates the win within a broader crisis of Chancellor Merz's government and the failure of the mainstream "firewall" strategy against the far right, warning of dangerous consequences for Germany and its European allies; across its pieces it also stresses the historical-moral dimension - framing the victory as a conscious repudiation of Germany's reckoning with its Nazi past - alongside the detachment and despair of German cultural and political elites, and the collapsing trust in left-wing parties across Germany, Europe and the West. The richest and most varied coverage comes from Süddeutsche Zeitung, which brings together many voices from within Germany itself: on-the-ground reporting on the fear among civil-society groups, migrants and minority advocates facing the AfD's proposed policies; an account of the CDU's decline in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern; opinion pieces urging citizens to respond with civic courage rather than banning the party or succumbing to pessimism; commentary framing the result as exposing an unresolved historical rupture between East and West Germany rooted in post-unification denial; reader letters drawing warning parallels to the Nazis' 1933 rise to power; and socioeconomic analyses arguing that economic insecurity and a sense of political neglect have turned protest voters into a durable base, with governing parties like the CDU and SPD blamed for having created the conditions for the AfD's success. Where the Telegraph offers a narrow, incident-driven account and the Guardian a national and pan-European moral-political interpretation, Süddeutsche Zeitung presents by far the most internally complex picture, weaving together local, historical and economic perspectives that reflect an intense domestic German debate over responsibility, fear and hope.

⌄

In sum, where the Telegraph worries about memory and education, the Guardian indicts the geopolitical and civil-liberties fallout of America's response, and the Wall Street Journal oscillates between patriotic tribute and domestic policy critique.

Where The Guardian spans intelligence reporting, parliamentary politics, and structural economic critique, The Wall Street Journal concentrates its coverage into an urgent, almost alarm-toned call to halt development immediately, reading more as a personal warning than a policy analysis.
