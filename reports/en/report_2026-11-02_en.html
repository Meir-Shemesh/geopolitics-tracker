Gen Z riots and cost-of-living protests across Germany, France and Europe

⌄

Coverage of the wave of unrest across Germany, France and Switzerland splits between sources that foreground official blame-laying and those that probe deeper social causes. The Daily Telegraph, in both its initial report and Freya Jones's fuller account, centres the story almost entirely on the German interior minister's claim that "leftists and migrants" were behind the violence, while a separate Telegraph opinion piece warns that legitimate cost-of-living protests are being hijacked by militants, undermining public sympathy. Neue Zürcher Zeitung echoes that framing in Anna Schiller's report quoting Minister Dobrindt, but broadens the lens considerably through Marius Huber's piece on a coordinated "European night of riots" spanning France, Germany and Switzerland, Christine Longin's report on renewed French unrest that criticizes excessive police force against minors, Robin Schwarzenbach's account of a violent night in Zurich, and Christina Neuhaus's investigation into networked left-extremist cells carrying out cross-border revenge attacks. Süddeutsche Zeitung offers the most varied treatment: Sina-Maria Schweikle's factual account of protests turning violent in 34 German cities sits alongside Gerhard Matzig's reflective essay on protest reshaping urban space, and Peter Laudenbach's interview with sociologist Wilhelm Heitmeyer, who argues that social inequality and loss of control - not mere "brutalization" - explain the violence, and who cautions against political distrust of youth movements. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung strikes a similar balance, pairing Alexander Jürgs's factual report on the Frankfurt escalation with Reinhard Müller's column warning that violence risks justifying bans on protest altogether, doing a disservice to young people's legitimate grievances. Die Welt, through Philipp Woldin, emphasizes political calls for tougher consequences and officials' framing of the rioters' migrant background. By contrast, The Washington Post, The Guardian, Los Angeles Times and Globe and Mail - via Victoria Bisset, Jon Henley, and the team of Angela Charlton and Kirsten Grieshaber - take a more panoramic, outside view, stressing the pan-European scale of the unrest across Germany, France, Portugal, Switzerland and Albania over affordability, housing and education, and documenting mass arrests and threatened crackdowns, without engaging deeply in the domestic German debate over who the rioters actually were.

North Korean hack of Binance crypto exchange

⌄

All seven sources report the same core event - a record $6.3 to $6.43 billion hack of the crypto exchange Binance, which the FBI attributes to the North Korean-linked group "TraderTraitor" - but they diverge in emphasis. The Daily Telegraph, in Matthew Field's report, and The Guardian both foreground the immediate financial fallout: a wave of roughly £2.7-3 billion in investor withdrawals, alongside Binance's pledge to absorb the losses itself. The Globe and Mail, in David Yaffe-Bellany's investigative piece, offers the most detailed reconstruction of how the breach was actually carried out, framing it as the largest crypto heist in history. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung concentrates on the FBI's formal attribution and its call for companies to block specific wallet addresses to curb money laundering, while Süddeutsche Zeitung adds a distinct political angle, contrasting the FBI's firm attribution to North Korea with Trump's notably more cautious stance on confirming the link, and also noting that Binance has already returned some 95% of the stolen funds. Die Welt, in a piece by Benedikt Fuest, supplies the most striking technical detail - that the entire breach originated from a single compromised laptop - and situates the theft within the broadest geopolitical frame, noting that US officials say the stolen funds help finance North Korea's nuclear and missile programs despite international sanctions. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in Michael Ferber's report, moves beyond the single incident to place it within an escalating pattern of destructive cyberattacks, detailing specifically how the stolen funds were laundered through stablecoins and lending platforms.

Together, these pieces range from professional military assessment, through a political call for European responsibility and an analytical warning against complacency, to cultural-psychological analysis and grassroots youth mobilization - all orbiting the same underlying Russian threat.

Poland's constitutional/judicial crisis

⌄

All three German-language outlets cover the dramatic clash between Polish President Nawrocki and Prime Minister Tusk over the Constitutional Tribunal, but differ in focus and tone. Süddeutsche Zeitung offers the most vivid eyewitness-style account of the episode itself - Nawrocki's physical storming of the court building - first in a straight news report, then in two columns by Viktoria Großmann, who brands the president a "hooligan" and warns that the incident sets a dangerous precedent and should serve as a cautionary tale for Europeans to shore up democratic institutions against illiberal forces; the same piece also quotes Tusk presenting himself as restoring the rule of law after years of "PiS injustice." Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung situates the standoff within a broader institutional narrative: one report frames the escalation as another chapter in the destruction of the rule of law by PiS, Duda, and Nawrocki, noting that former court presidents have sided with Tusk's government, while another piece, bylined by Stefan Locke and built around the striking image of Nawrocki forcing entry with an angle grinder, stresses that a constitutional state differs from "the law of the jungle" precisely because justice is not decided by physical force - a warning implicitly aimed at both sides. In a separate column for Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Locke goes further, arguing paradoxically that PiS's earlier breach of the rule of law (its partisan packing of the court) can now seemingly only be remedied through a further breach - Nawrocki's refusal to swear in duly elected judges and Tusk's own questionable countermeasures - leaving legal peace the casualty either way. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in Meret Baumann's report, takes the most measured stance among all the sources, stressing that both sides' actions are legally contested, that this does not amount to an actual coup, yet Poland has nonetheless been plunged into a severe crisis with no clear way out, requiring broad cross-party dialogue that neither side currently shows real willingness to pursue.

European far-right 'remigration' movement

⌄
