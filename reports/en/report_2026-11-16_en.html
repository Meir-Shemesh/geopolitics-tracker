Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral summit in Shenzhen

⌄

Coverage of the planned Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral summit in Shenzhen splits between straight reporting, invocations of a "new Yalta," and skepticism about substantive outcomes. The Guardian provides the baseline factual report of the planned meeting, and in a separate analysis traces how the Yalta comparison has gained currency among commentators, while cautioning that a modern-day carve-up deciding Ukraine's and Taiwan's fates without their involvement is unlikely, and noting that Southeast Asian nations are hedging against over-dependence on Washington. Süddeutsche Zeitung, in a piece by Charlotte Walser, similarly highlights fears among European and American experts of a new Yalta-style division of spheres of influence at Ukraine's and Europe's expense, though it notes experts are split on whether the summit poses more risk or opportunity for Washington. In a letter to the Daily Telegraph, Francis Bown sharpens this anxiety considerably, warning that Trump - weakened by his "foolish wars" in Iran and Ukraine - risks being outmanoeuvred by Xi and Putin. Writing in the Globe and Mail, Charles Burton goes further still, warning the summit could normalize a club of authoritarian strongmen coordinating to carve up spheres of influence at the expense of Taiwan, Ukraine and middle powers like Canada, and urges Ottawa to deepen ties with the EU and Indo-Pacific democracies rather than rely on Washington. By contrast, Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in reporting by Matthias Kamp, and the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in an analysis by Bernhard Bartsch, take a more sober view: Kamp predicts the "historic" summit will mainly produce "nice pictures" rather than concrete results, while Bartsch argues explicitly that Shenzhen will not be a "second Yalta," since China prefers bilateral deals within a multilateral framework and other Asia-Pacific countries are wary of domination by the "big three." Meanwhile, the Daily Telegraph, in reporting by Phelim Kine, and the Los Angeles Times, in two separate pieces including one by Michael Wilner, focus less on the summit itself and more on the perception that Trump has been politically weakened by midterm losses, with Asian leaders reportedly planning to "mollify" and "string him along" rather than concede to his demands, and with longtime U.S. allies in the region voicing alarm that they are being sidelined in a great-power bargain.

German deindustrialization and Mercedes crisis

⌄

All three sources treat German deindustrialization and the Mercedes situation with concern, but differ sharply in emphasis and tone. Where Poschardt casts the story as a political failure and celebrates confrontation with unions, Welp focuses on institutional adaptability amid Chinese competition, and Marten offers a macroeconomic caution against mistaking state-driven growth for genuine recovery.
