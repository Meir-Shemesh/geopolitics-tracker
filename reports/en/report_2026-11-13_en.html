Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang espionage scandal and resignation

⌄

Nearly every major outlet covers the same core event - Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's abrupt resignation amid an affair with Alice Huang, a woman US officials suspect of being a Chinese intelligence asset tied to Megaspeed, a firm accused of helping smuggle restricted chips to China - but the framing diverges sharply. The Economist takes the most forceful editorial stance, running several opinion pieces insisting bluntly that Huang "must go," while another Economist piece widens the blame to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's lax enforcement and even to Trump himself, and yet another frames the affair as potentially "the most expensive shag in history" for its corporate fallout. The Washington Post builds the most extensive factual investigation, covering America's failure to assist Singapore's probe, congressional demands for deeper scrutiny, Nvidia's first-ever board chair appointment, and a bot-driven social-media campaign defending Huang; in a column for the paper, Craig Singleton argues chipmakers cannot be trusted to police themselves on China, while an unsigned piece warns the episode exposed dangerously discretionary power held by Lutnick. The Guardian offers two distinct angles: in his column, Nils Pratley argues Huang's fall was a predictable "busted flush" given his reckless personal conduct, while in a separate piece Aisha Down probes whether the AI economy can function without the opaque web of relationships Huang personally built, and a third Guardian piece serves as an explainer for readers. Der Spiegel provides the deepest personal profiling, portraying Huang in one piece as an unaccountable "patriarch" running a nearly four-trillion-dollar company, leaving open whether his wife is truly a spy, while another piece focuses on how his closeness to Trump shielded him politically. At Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, Winand von Petersdorff's article relays a short-seller's detailed claims that Huang knowingly enabled chip diversion, while a separate FAZ piece profiles Alice Huang herself and Beijing's denials. Neue Zürcher Zeitung stands out for a contrarian angle: in a column by Markus Städeli, Huang's ouster is framed as actually lucky for Nvidia and the US AI industry by forcing more conservative risk management, alongside a detailed chronology and a profile of an analyst who predicted the crisis. The Globe and Mail and Los Angeles Times focus mainly on economic fallout - fears of an AI bubble bursting and Lutnick's own shaky standing in the White House. Süddeutsche Zeitung adds the political dimension of Democratic senators demanding record preservation, while USA Today and Die Welt offer more straightforwardly factual accounts of the resignation and its espionage backdrop.

⌄

COP31 climate summit disagreements

⌄

Coverage of COP31 in Antalya splits into three distinct angles. The Globe and Mail and USA Today, in near-identical reports by Simon Jessop (with co-authors), offer straightforward accounts of the clash between small island states demanding urgent emissions cuts, European nations pushing electrification, and fossil-fuel producers like Venezuela defending their right to keep developing oil and gas - both organizing their coverage around the same quoted warning that "the planet is not dying, it is being killed." By contrast, The Guardian, the Los Angeles Times and Süddeutsche Zeitung center their coverage on Greta Thunberg's return to the international stage and her activist critique. In The Guardian, Oliver Milman reports Thunberg accusing the very governments meant to "rescue" the planet of killing it, while also noting John Kerry's rebuke of Trump and the US withdrawal from climate cooperation.
