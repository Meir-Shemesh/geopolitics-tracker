Israeli election and Netanyahu's political fate

⌄

Coverage of Israel's election diverges sharply in emphasis and tone. The Daily Telegraph, in Henry Bodkin's report, frames Netanyahu as fighting literally for his freedom - "victory or prison" - and highlights fear-based campaigning, including an AI-generated image of a nuclear strike on Tel Aviv; a companion Telegraph piece by Paul Nuki casts Gadi Eisenkot's rise as a potential historic break from Ashkenazi political dominance. Süddeutsche Zeitung touches the same ethnic angle from a different direction: in a column, Ronen Steinke traces the bureaucratic, humiliating origins of Jewish surnames like Eisenkot, while in another column Moshe Sakal, an Israeli-German writer, voices skepticism that ousting Netanyahu would resolve Israel's deeper problems of occupation and political paralysis. The Guardian, through Emma Graham-Harrison's reporting (some co-bylined with Julian Borger), takes the most pointed accusatory line, portraying Netanyahu as fearmongering over Iran and pressuring the military toward confrontation on the eve of a tight vote, framed around his apocalyptic rhetoric about an alliance between "the left and Islam." Die Welt, in a piece by Daniel-Dylan Böhmer, offers a more cautious version of the same suspicion, noting that Israeli military circles worry Netanyahu might exploit Iran tensions electorally without asserting this as established fact. By contrast, The Washington Post and Los Angeles Times focus less on personal motive and more on political mechanics: the Post reports that Netanyahu could lose the popular vote yet still retain power via a unity government with Eisenkot, stressing Trump's unprecedented public intervention on Netanyahu's behalf, while the Los Angeles Times, through Josef Federman, frames the election as a referendum on Netanyahu's handling of October 7 and its aftermath, and in a separate piece by Melanie Lidman documents eroding Likud loyalty in the north amid lingering war with Hezbollah. USA Today, via Kim Hjelmgaard, offers a more balanced reckoning with Netanyahu's security legacy as the central campaign question. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung provides a multi-layered portrait: one piece maps possible coalition scenarios, Daniel Mahla documents existential anxiety driving Israelis to seek German passports as a safety net, and Itay Goder traces the ethnic political rift back to the history of the Beitar Jerusalem football club. Neue Zürcher Zeitung, through Johannes Bockenheimer, assembles a polarized chorus of Israeli voices without pressing its own argument, while Süddeutsche Zeitung, in a further piece by Sina-Maria Schweikle, examines how a Netanyahu defeat might open new diplomatic room for Germany's Israel policy.

Brazil presidential election: Lula's victory and aftermath

⌄

Coverage of Lula's victory over Flávio Bolsonaro ranges from celebratory to deeply skeptical. The Guardian offers the most positive framing, portraying "Lula's army" digital mobilization as a triumph over the far right and quoting international leaders hailing the result as a victory for democracy against "dictatorship and fascism," while also stressing that it represents a defeat for perceived US interference aimed at turning Brazil into Washington's "backyard." The Washington Post and the Los Angeles Times, in a piece by Isabel Debre, similarly frame the outcome mainly in geopolitical terms, as a setback for Trump and the MAGA-aligned rightward wave sweeping Latin America, with Brazil now positioned as a counterweight to US dominance in the region. Süddeutsche Zeitung strikes a split tone: in one column Benedikt Peters argues Trump was denied his regional "main prize," while in another Jan Heidtmann credits Lula's win as deserving respect given the alternative, yet questions whether an exhausted veteran politician still has answers to Brazil's deep problems. By contrast, Die Welt, in columns by Tobias Käufer, and Neue Zürcher Zeitung, in a column by Simon Hehli, take a more critical view of Lula himself. Käufer attributes Bolsonaro's loss to his own lack of charisma rather than any real weakness of the conservative camp, pointing to Tarcísio de Freitas as a stronger future alternative, while Hehli explicitly argues there is "no reason to celebrate" given Lula's ties to Putin, Maduro and Cuba, even while conceding that a Bolsonaro presidency would have posed a greater danger. The Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, in a column by Nikolas Busse, likewise cautions against celebrating the result as a global "democratic" win, noting Brazil's continued engagement with the anti-Western BRICS bloc. The Daily Telegraph stands out for prominently reporting Donald Trump's claim that the election was "rigged" despite a clear vote margin, alongside Eva Hartog's analysis of Brazil's deep polarization and lingering risk of political instability. The market crash following Lula's win is covered in broadly similar terms by The Daily Telegraph, the Globe and Mail, and the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung (in a piece by Gregor Brunner, who draws a contrast with investors' relatively greater confidence in Israel's stability despite its own turmoil), all emphasizing investor distrust of Lula's fiscal plans. Meanwhile, Süddeutsche Zeitung and the Washington Post, in reporting by Terrence McCoy, both dig into the mechanics of Lula's social-media "army" and grassroots mobilization that powered his runoff comeback.

⌄
