Rise of the hard right in Europe (Sweden, AfD)

⌄

Coverage of the hard right's rise in Europe splits into several distinct angles across the outlets. The Daily Telegraph frames the story geopolitically: one piece treats Sweden's election as a discrete political drama about the Sweden Democrats' advance toward power, while a separate opinion piece links AfD's rise to Kremlin propaganda, arguing that Russia's "Nazism" rhetoric echoes the 1939 Nazi-Soviet pact era. Die Welt offers the most sustained domestic analysis, approaching the German story from three complementary angles: one commentary argues AfD's success reflects a decades-long clash between liberalizing forces and a counter-movement prizing order, security and belonging, drawing parallels to Trump, Meloni and Tusk; another focuses on politician Ulrich Siegmund's relatable public image as the key to AfD's appeal, contrasting it with rivals who fail to project "we are like you"; and a third treats the Saxony-Anhalt election result as a genuine caesura for German democracy, blaming eastern distrust of the state while also cautioning against simply branding voters fascist rather than appealing to their reason. The Guardian, finally, diverges ideologically from all the others by turning the causal arrow inward: rather than dwelling on far-right tactics or Kremlin ties, it argues that the liberal centre's own embrace of neoliberal, "third way" politics hollowed out democracy's foundations and thereby enabled AfD's rise, locating the root cause in mainstream politics rather than in external propaganda or the far right's internal dynamics.

⌄
