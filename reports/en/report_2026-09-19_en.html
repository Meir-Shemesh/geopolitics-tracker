AI safety regulation debates

⌄

Coverage of AI safety regulation splits along fairly clear ideological lines. The Guardian frames the UK government as slow and disorganised, arguing that meaningful regulation is impossible without international coordination, especially between the US and China; its letters page also carries a more skeptical strand suggesting that AI companies themselves cultivate fear of future "superintelligence" to deflect attention from present-day harms and accountability. Los Angeles Times is the most forceful advocate for regulation, welcoming Gavin Newsom's reversal toward a California "kill switch" law, calling for a global AI treaty modeled on nuclear arms control, and repeatedly attacking Donald Trump for dismissing public AI-safety fears as a "hoax," arguing his hands-off stance serves industry insiders over ordinary Americans. The Washington Post takes a more measured, policy-oriented approach: Fareed Zakaria argues the "race ahead versus pause" framing misses the point and calls instead for mandatory testing and human oversight that wouldn't disadvantage the US against China, while a separate piece criticizes China's draconian exit bans on tech talent and warns the US against mimicking authoritarian tactics. The Wall Street Journal is the most skeptical of sweeping regulation: pieces there argue a US-China safety pact is unverifiable and thus unsafe, that uncertainty about AI's trajectory counsels vigilance rather than a pause, and that companies like OpenAI and Anthropic would benefit more from shareholder discipline than ideological restraint - though the paper is internally divided, since Peggy Noonan's column takes AI leaders' own fears seriously and accuses the industry of hiding real dangers behind euphemistic language.
