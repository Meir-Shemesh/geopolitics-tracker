AfD election victory in Germany and its aftermath

⌄

Coverage of the AfD's landslide win in Saxony-Anhalt reveals sharply different emphases between the German and Anglo-American press. Süddeutsche Zeitung offers by far the widest and most multifaceted array of angles: pieces framing the result as a historic "turning point" in which a far-right party stands for the first time on the threshold of governing power, alongside sharply focused analyses of the security risk posed by a potential AfD interior minister gaining control over the domestic intelligence service, warnings of capital and population flight from the state, criticism of the extreme underrepresentation of women in the AfD's parliamentary group, alarm over the possible collapse of German support for Ukraine, scrutiny of the BSW's ambiguous ties to both the AfD and Russia, a Jewish-community reckoning tied to Holocaust remembrance, and a survey of international reactions ranging from celebration by the global right (Austria's FPÖ, Hungary's Fidesz, Reform UK, the Trump administration) to alarm in France and Israel. Süddeutsche Zeitung's readers also weigh in fiercely, rejecting any journalism that reduces democracy to a mere arithmetic majority game and demanding a formal ban on the party. Die Welt, by contrast, turns its critical gaze inward, arguing that Germany's political, cultural and media elites bear responsibility for the AfD's rise through arrogance and detachment from ordinary citizens, and warning that continued elite denial is more dangerous than the AfD itself; it also surveys alarmed reactions from neighboring France and Poland over the party's Russia ties, presents SPD politician Ralf Stegner's forceful refusal to treat the AfD as a "normal party," and uniquely reports on an internal AfD debate over softening anti-Islam rhetoric to court Muslim voters - an angle found in no other source. The Daily Telegraph centers its account on Chancellor Merz's refusal to resign after the defeat, giving considerable weight to Russian interference as a driver of Germany's democratic crisis. The Wall Street Journal frames the story primarily as an institutional political analysis of Merz's weakened standing and mounting pressure within his own conservative camp to break the "firewall" against the AfD, treating the international and Russia-linked dimension more lightly than the German outlets do.

Sources: The Daily Telegraph, Die Welt, Süddeutsche Zeitung, The Wall Street Journal

Iran conflict: war, sanctions and domestic politics

⌄

Coverage of the Iranian crisis splits along quite different axes across the outlets. The Wall Street Journal devotes the most attention to the economic and strategic dimension from a US vantage point: two pieces describe how the American naval blockade and sanctions are drying up Iran's oil revenue and deepening its economic crisis, while a third exposes a loophole - Iranian money flowing through US correspondent banks - relevant to judging how effective the sanctions campaign really is. Beyond this, two Journal opinion pieces view the conflict through a strategic-historical lens: one urges Trump to secure a decisive victory in Iran, drawing on Bush's experience in Iraq, and the other compares George Washington's Fabian tactics to the attritional strategy of Iran's Revolutionary Guard - both reflecting an American preoccupation with the war's protraction and the need to justify it. By contrast, the Los Angeles Times offers a more critical, globally-minded socioeconomic frame, arguing that while investors and military contractors - including Trump-linked businesses - profit from the war, ordinary consumers and the world's poor, for instance in Sudan, bear the brunt of higher costs and hunger. Die Welt and The Daily Telegraph focus on operational and regional fallout: Die Welt reports on Iran's plan to establish a blockade zone near the Strait of Hormuz and sanction ships that violate it, while The Daily Telegraph extends this concern to Europe, warning of a severe gas crisis stemming from disruptions to Hormuz supply routes. The Daily Telegraph is also the only outlet to address Iranian domestic politics, reporting on former president Rouhani's call for a referendum to end the war alongside the fierce hardliner backlash against him - a angle highlighting internal fractures within the Iranian regime found in no other source.

Migration failures fueling European far-right rise

⌄

Three of the papers approach the same underlying story - migration failures fueling the far right - from distinct angles. The Daily Telegraph adopts a warning tone, pointing to Channel crossings as evidence of enforcement failure in both Britain and Germany, and implies that continued government inaction will push citizens toward vigilante responses and boost parties like the AfD, effectively arguing for firmer border enforcement. The Guardian, across two separate pieces, offers a more structural analysis: one article argues that the AfD's historic result in Saxony-Anhalt reflects a decade-long European trend driven not by voters shifting ideologically but by mainstream parties' failure to deliver and their normalisation of far-right rhetoric - noting that keeping the AfD out of power has actually strengthened it by shielding it from the compromises of governing. The second Guardian piece extends this logic to Britain, suggesting Reform UK could outlast Farage because it taps into a deeper public distrust in democracy's capacity to solve problems, with migration serving merely as the visible symptom of that broader malaise. The Wall Street Journal takes the widest lens, folding migration pressures into a larger indictment of European fragmentation in the face of geopolitical threats from Russia and China, warning that the continent is "hitting the snooze button" instead of confronting a spreading crisis. So while the Telegraph stresses immediate enforcement failure, the Guardian probes the structural political roots of far-right growth, and the Journal situates migration within a broader narrative of European strategic disunity.
