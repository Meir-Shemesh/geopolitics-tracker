US-Canada trade war and tariffs

⌄

Coverage of the US-Canada trade war splits along clear editorial lines. The Economist is the most consistently cautionary voice: across a run of pieces it grants that Carney's retaliatory tariffs are proportionate and domestically popular, yet repeatedly warns that the confrontation could spiral out of control, that Canada is far more exposed than the US and therefore cannot "win," and that even leverage points like restricting energy exports carry serious self-harm risk, including inflaming Alberta's separatist movement. The New York Times International takes the opposite stance, framing Carney's refusal to bow to Trump as a historic assertion of Canadian sovereignty against American domineering tactics and celebrating a national identity of toughness against a "transactional" Trump worldview. Los Angeles Times adopts a more reportorial tone but devotes the most space to concrete economic fallout - from California's wine industry to a long list of goods hit by 50% tariffs - while also foregrounding Carney's framing of the dispute as a test of whether "middle powers" can resist coercion, quoting his defiant line that "America is trying to break us." The Wall Street Journal focuses on pragmatic economic criticism of Trump himself, calling the escalating tariffs on Canadian autos and goods "the dumbest trade war," one that hurts US manufacturers more than Canada and undercuts Trump politically. USA Today centers on tangible consumer-price effects on both sides of the border, quoting Carney's charge that the US "asked too much, and offered too little." The Guardian emphasizes the apparent futility of negotiating with the Trump administration and Canada's willingness to let talks collapse rather than compromise on Quebec's French-language protections. Der Spiegel, by contrast, writes from an explicitly European, prescriptive angle, holding up Carney as a model other leaders - especially in Europe - should emulate, arguing that submission only emboldens Trump and that his current political weakness should be exploited rather than appeased.

US sanctions campaign on Iran ('Operation Economic Outcast')

⌄

Most of the coverage - especially the extensive Economist series - converges on a shared conclusion: the new American sanctions campaign against Iran, branded "Operation Economic Outcast" and an "economic D-Day," is unlikely to topple the Iranian regime. But the outlets diverge sharply in explaining why and in their broader framing. The Economist offers the most developed analysis, arguing that Washington is reluctant to impose full secondary sanctions for fear of "blowing up the global financial system" (in Treasury Secretary Bessent's own words), that the real target of the pressure is China as Iran's chief oil buyer, and that the campaign may perversely strengthen the Revolutionary Guard, which profits from smuggling and import substitution, rather than weakening the regime. Several Economist pieces go further, suggesting the actual goal may not be regime collapse at all but avoiding another round of military escalation. The Wall Street Journal instead emphasizes the gap between tough rhetoric and realities on the ground: its reporting from Dubai shows deep Iran-UAE commercial ties persisting unhindered, while another piece details the shadow shipping networks, yuan settlements and crypto transactions Tehran uses to outlast the pressure; a Journal editorial, by contrast, backs the sanctions push but warns its success hinges on Trump actually following through against enablers like the UAE and China rather than retreating. USA Today foregrounds the campaign's militarized rhetoric - framing it as an "economic onslaught" with explicit threats against third countries - and adds a detailed look at Iraq as a test case for U.S. leverage, alongside reporting on Iran's preserved missile capabilities despite the pressure. The Los Angeles Times, echoing The Economist's skepticism, warns the strategy could backfire by provoking Iranian retaliation across the Gulf, while also giving voice to Iranian President Pezeshkian, who defends the earlier U.S.-Iran memorandum as a necessary step rather than capitulation. The Guardian largely relays the American government's own triumphant framing of the campaign as a historic turning point, without taking a strong independent stance, while still flagging the same uncertainty other outlets raise over whether Washington will actually confront China.

Overall, The Guardian emphasizes human toll and foreign backing, Der Spiegel exposes a specific lethal tactic, The Wall Street Journal analyzes the two-way strategic dynamic, and the Los Angeles Times examines the internal economic pressure building inside Russia.

Trump's immigration crackdown and mass deportations

⌄

Coverage of this topic is dominated overwhelmingly by The Economist, which devoted an extensive, consistent series of pieces to a firmly critical editorial line - opposing Trump's immigration crackdown not primarily on humanitarian grounds but on grounds of economic and practical self-harm. Its articles trace how the promised "largest deportation operation in history" has drifted from targeting recent border-crossers and criminals to sweeping up long-settled immigrants, families and holders of Temporary Protected Status, producing deaths in detention, aggressive tactics like flashbang raids, and daily arrest quotas that strain the rule of law. A parallel economic strand argues that the crackdown - squeezing everyone from undocumented laborers to skilled H-1B workers and foreign students - is shrinking the workforce, slowing growth, and ceding America's competitive edge in talent to China, Canada and the EU. Die Welt strikes a narrower, more technical note, focusing critical attention on the "ICE Air" deportation airline as a bungled, overpriced procurement effort whose practical viability is doubtful - a bureaucratic-cost critique rather than a broader moral or political indictment of the policy itself. Los Angeles Times, by contrast, shifts the story away from policy mechanics and economics toward its human and cultural fallout: one report documents Mexico's escalating diplomatic pressure over the deaths of its citizens in ICE custody and Washington's failure to respond adequately, while a related cultural piece frames Dolly Parton's cover of "Deportee" as a compassionate tribute to immigrant laborers that stands in stark moral contrast to the anti-immigrant rhetoric of Trump and JD Vance. Taken together, where The Economist assesses the crackdown mainly through the lens of economic efficiency and legal order, Los Angeles Times and Die Welt each add complementary critical angles - one humanitarian and moral, the other operational and financial - highlighting costs of the same policy that go largely unaddressed elsewhere.
