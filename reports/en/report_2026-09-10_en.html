Iran-US conflict in Strait of Hormuz and oil impact

⌄

Three of the four pieces on the Hormuz standoff come from The Wall Street Journal, and together they function as a single, consistent voice: a strategic, war-focused analysis centered on Iran's internal logic. That analysis argues that Iran, squeezed by the U.S. naval blockade and crippling new sanctions, is deliberately escalating attacks on shipping and American allies to break the impasse, since the Strait remains its "jewel in the crown" - its last real point of leverage. From that same vantage point comes the report on internal White House unease, with officials privately fearing a years-long conflict even as Trump publicly promises imminent victory. The Los Angeles Times, by contrast, takes an economic rather than military angle, focusing on the immediate consequences for ordinary Americans: oil prices topping $100 a barrel and gas prices jumping, while warning that a durable resolution before the U.S. midterms - or even after - looks increasingly unlikely. So where the Journal dissects the strategic motives and anxieties of the political and military actors, the Los Angeles Times translates that same stalemate into its tangible impact on markets and consumers.

9/11 legacy: wars, legal accountability, and politics

⌄

The two papers approach the legacy of 9/11 from starkly opposed political vantage points. Los Angeles Times turns a critical eye inward on American power and institutions: one piece argues that Trump, despite criticizing Bush's Iraq war, has repeated the same mistakes by launching an unauthorized war against Iran that weakens US standing while China and Russia exploit the distraction and domestic security institutions are undermined; a companion piece extends that critique to the legal realm, arguing that US courts have repeatedly failed to hold the executive branch accountable for torture and rendition, and that this impunity persists under Trump - amounting to a call for structural reform of checks and balances. The Wall Street Journal, by contrast, aims its criticism outward and at domestic political rivals rather than at US policy itself: one column accuses European commentators of reflexively blaming America and second-guessing its response to terrorism, applying what it calls a premodern moral double standard compared with how they treat attacks on France or Israel; another editorial targets New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, accusing him of pushing revisionist, conspiratorial claims about Ground Zero air-quality cover-ups for political gain. Where the Los Angeles Times frames 9/11's legacy as a story of America's own policy and legal failures, The Wall Street Journal frames it as a story of unfair foreign criticism and opportunistic domestic politicians exploiting the tragedy.
