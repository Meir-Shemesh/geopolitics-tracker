Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral summit in Shenzhen

⌄

Nearly every outlet covering the first-ever trilateral summit between Trump, Xi and Putin in Shenzhen agrees it was heavy on symbolism and light on substance, but they diverge sharply on who actually won. The Daily Telegraph runs a spread of angles: Joe Barnes reports straightforwardly on the joint pledges over energy, the UN and the ICC, while Adrian Blomfield argues Putin craved symbolic equality more than real gains, and David Blair with Roland Oliphant map out each leader's divergent objectives and partial wins. In his column for the paper, Ben Wallace goes further, branding Trump and Putin "lame ducks" and accusing Trump of abandoning democratic values to flatter a "war criminal", a harshness echoed in the paper's published letters and its editorial, which likewise concede Putin a symbolic win while doubting sanctions relief is practically enforceable. Die Welt offers a comparable range: unsigned factual reporting on the joint declaration sits alongside Christina zur Nedden's and others' analyses concluding Xi Jinping is the clear winner for hosting both nuclear powers without conceding anything, while Pavel Lokshin, in his column, argues Putin dreamed of a "Yalta 2.0" but secured little of substance, and Annett Meiritz, in hers, instead turns the lens on Europe's "helpless" marginalization. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung pairs straight reporting with Berthold Kohler's pointed column branding the three leaders autocratic "brothers in spirit," and also imports translated commentary from the Financial Times, Jyllands-Posten and La Vanguardia offering further takes on Putin's weakness and each leader's need for a domestic PR win. Süddeutsche Zeitung, across pieces by Daniel Brössler and Reymer Klüver, David Pfeifer and colleagues, and Silke Bigalke, stresses the gap between the summit's grand imagery and its thin results, casting Xi as stage director and Putin as the biggest symbolic beneficiary. The Economist expands the frame furthest beyond the summit itself: alongside a factual report and a piece arguing the meeting dented American presidential authority even as it boosted Trump's personal historical profile, it runs an extensive, near-identical series of pieces contending that, beyond Shenzhen, a broader "axis of malice" among Russia, China, Iran and North Korea is deepening and threatens the Western-led order. The Washington Post and Los Angeles Times, via Cat Zakrzewski and colleagues and Michael Wilner respectively, emphasize a politically weakened Trump whose ambitions outpaced his leverage, while The Globe and Mail's James Griffiths highlights that the joint declaration never even mentioned Ukraine or Iran. USA Today combines straight reporting with sharp criticism from Ukrainian and European officials who branded the summit a "historic embarrassment" and a betrayal for sidelining Ukraine entirely.

ICJ genocide ruling against Myanmar

⌄

Most outlets, including The Economist, Los Angeles Times, USA Today, The Washington Post, Neue Zürcher Zeitung and The Globe and Mail, cover the ICJ's landmark ruling as straightforward legal reporting: for the first time a state, Myanmar, has been held responsible for genocide, over the 2017 mass killing and sexual violence against the Rohingya, with the court ordering reparations and non-repetition. The Globe and Mail adds a distinct political angle by reporting the UN Secretary-General's statement that the ruling is binding and final, and by noting the likely objection of the Trump administration, which fears the precedent could be used against American soldiers and allies. Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung offers the most varied coverage: alongside straight reporting (including by Finn Hohenschwert) on the verdict and its precedent-setting nature, it runs a column by Reinhard Müller warning that every state, including Israel, already facing proceedings in The Hague, must fear this precedent, while cautioning against turning genocide accusations into a political weapon, as well as a biographical piece by Reinhard Veser on Raphael Lemkin, who coined the term "genocide," tracing the historical political resistance to the concept. The sharpest framing comes from The Daily Telegraph: beyond Sarah Newey and Paul Nuki's factual report on the verdict itself, the same authors publish a separate piece arguing explicitly that the court's legal reasoning in the Myanmar case, particularly its reliance on circumstantial evidence and its rejection of counterinsurgency as a defense, creates a "template" that makes it more likely genocidal intent could be established against Israel over Gaza. Süddeutsche Zeitung, in Arne Perras's article, strikes a more tempered note, stressing that despite the ruling's historic significance, Russia and China retain the ability to shield Myanmar's military junta, meaning the verdict has little immediate practical effect on power within the country.

Russia-Ukraine bridge and infrastructure strikes

⌄

All the sources cover the same core event, repeated Ukrainian drone and missile strikes on the Kerch bridge, but frame its significance differently. Die Welt and Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung offer tightly factual reporting: the former describes the destruction of the bridge's central road section, while the latter, in a piece bylined by Thomas Gutschker and Friedrich Schmidt, adds technical detail about Dutch-made Ruta missiles and the role of the company Destinus in supplying Ukraine, stressing that Russia has effectively lost the ability to resupply its forces in southern Ukraine via this route. The Guardian and The Economist amplify the Ukrainian framing of the strike as "a symbolic blow to the Kremlin," with The Economist going furthest in a multi-part analysis asking who is winning the mutual bridge-bombing campaign, arguing that while the Kerch bridge strike is a serious symbolic and logistical setback for Putin, Russia's own strikes on Kyiv's bridges may ultimately matter more strategically, alongside an assessment of Ukraine's increasingly precise strikes set against Russia's crude but damaging ballistic missile attacks. By contrast, in a column for Süddeutsche Zeitung, Florian Hassel cautions against overstating Ukraine's success, arguing that destroying the bridge's rail link settles nothing, since Russia continues to pound Ukrainian infrastructure heavily, and the real question remains how long Ukraine can withstand the pressure of a hard winter. A more diplomacy-focused angle appears in the Los Angeles Times and The Washington Post, both of which link the bridge strike to the parallel negotiation track: Putin vows retaliation and insists Russia "will not agree" to terms, while Trump tells European leaders he believes Putin wants peace. In a column for The Washington Post, David Ignatius highlights the gap between fast-moving military escalation and slow diplomacy, writing that "this process needs more diplomacy and fewer bombs," while The Economist notes that the Kremlin dismissed Jared Kushner's claims of progress and rejected any European role in the talks.

UK-EU relations and Brexit reset under Burnham

⌄

Coverage of Andy Burnham's push for a post-Brexit reset with the EU splits sharply along source lines. The Daily Telegraph runs a cluster of skeptical pieces: in a column, Dominic Lawson argues the EU wants closer UK ties purely because it needs British money, not out of goodwill, while in a separate column Szu Ping Chan points to upwardly revised productivity data as proof Britain is thriving outside the bloc, undercutting the economic case for reset. Straight news pieces in the same paper report Scottish fishing leaders' fear that Burnham will trade away their waters by re-entering the Common Fisheries Policy, and note his openness to a Rwanda-style deportation pact with Brussels; a further Daily Telegraph report presents Labour's Stella Creasy advocating a cautious "Swiss-style" relationship rather than outright rejoining. By contrast, Der Spiegel, across three separate pieces, together with Neue Zürcher Zeitung and Die Welt, frame Burnham far more sympathetically, as a leader finally willing to state plainly that Brexit has done more harm than good. In a column for Neue Zürcher Zeitung, David Signer invokes the "emperor's new clothes" to describe the political cowardice that preceded Burnham, while in a piece for Die Welt, Claudia Wanner calls his move a "taboo-breaking" step that could eventually lead to a full "Breturn." The Guardian's factual report aligns with this sympathetic framing, emphasizing Burnham's claim that Britain's global standing has diminished since Brexit. Süddeutsche Zeitung adds both an academic and diplomatic angle: in an interview, historian Kiran Klaus Patel describes Brexit as a "festering wound" that has left Britain weakened and more vulnerable, while a separate report covers the concrete outcomes of a Brussels summit on trade, electricity markets and student exchanges, framing Brexit a decade on as an economic and geopolitical failure. The overall contrast is stark: German- and Swiss-based coverage largely endorses the reset as correcting a historic mistake, while The Daily Telegraph repeatedly warns it risks surrendering British sovereignty and resources.

EU migration policy and Rwanda return-hub deal

⌄

The Economist runs an extended series tracking the emerging deal in which Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands will send rejected asylum-seekers to a Rwandan "return hub," moving from straightforward reporting on the new EU legislation toward more critical analysis of its limits: one piece notes the scheme rests on firmer legal footing than Britain's failed 2022 Rwanda deal but questions whether it will meaningfully reduce the huge backlog of pending return orders, while others quote human-rights groups warning the arrangement "finances and legitimises" an abusive government, and still another frames it as part of centrist politicians' attempt to harden migration policy to head off right-wing populist gains. The Guardian similarly reports the scheme has already been denounced as "a grave moral failing," foregrounding the ethical backlash. The Daily Telegraph, by contrast, covers the story mainly through a British domestic lens, reporting Andy Burnham's openness to a Rwanda-style deportation deal with the EU as part of a post-Brexit reset, without engaging with the moral critiques that dominate the other sources' coverage. German-language commentary is sharply split: in a column in Die Welt, Christian Putsch argues that despite moral and practical objections, Germany's planned Rwanda deportation center is worth attempting as a deterrent, given Rwanda's relative stability compared with alternatives like Uganda and the mounting economic pressures driving migration from Africa. In direct contrast, in a column in Süddeutsche Zeitung, Heribert Prantl denounces the same plan, arguing that these "return hubs" amount to a euphemistic dismantling of the right to asylum in violation of the Geneva Convention and European human-rights law, warning they could become "a Guantánamo of the EU."

Gaza ceasefire violations and humanitarian crisis

⌄

Coverage of this topic splits sharply between a narrow, security-focused account and broader critiques of the ceasefire's humanitarian failures. Die Welt offers a brief, factual report on an Israeli ground operation to destroy a tunnel deemed an "immediate threat," framing the ceasefire breach as a targeted, justified security measure without engaging with wider humanitarian consequences. Together, the sources range from Die Welt's largely uncritical security framing, through Los Angeles Times's documentary emphasis on children's suffering, to The Guardian's and The Economist's pointed criticism of Israeli policy toward Gaza.
