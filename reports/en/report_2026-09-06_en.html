Israel-Palestine: soft power and settler violence

⌄

The two pieces address entirely different facets of Israel's international image, each from its own angle. The Daily Telegraph focuses on the soft-power dimension, praising the television series "Fauda" as an effective "weapon" against rising global anti-Zionism, arguing it humanizes both Israelis and Palestinians and functions as cultural diplomacy that resonates internationally despite boycott campaigns. The Los Angeles Times, by contrast, strikes a far darker and more concrete note: it reports that U.S. Ambassador Mike Huckabee - a figure generally supportive of Israel - explicitly labeled West Bank settler violence "terrorism" rather than mere criminality, citing attacks on Palestinian Americans and the broader international consensus that the settlements themselves are illegal. The contrast is stark: where the Telegraph celebrates Israel's soft-power successes in culture and media, the Los Angeles Times shows that on the ground, even close allies find it hard to ignore or downplay settler violence, condemning it in unusually forceful terms.

UK fiscal crisis, debt and taxation

⌄

All seven articles on this topic come from the Daily Telegraph alone, forming a unified chorus that attacks the Labour government's fiscal policy from several complementary angles. Some pieces center on warnings from economists and market voices - Arthur Laffer argues Britain is "taxing itself to death" and needs tax cuts on work rather than further rises, while another article cites bond-market experts warning that Britain is paying a "credibility premium" as investors lose faith in its fiscal management. Other pieces take a more ideological-commentary angle: one attacks Andy Burnham and Labour's left wing for refusing to confront fiscal reality while praising Reform UK and Conservative plans for spending and tax cuts; another, by Daniel Hannan, frames the national debt as Britain's foremost strategic weakness, eroding its military and diplomatic standing before rivals; and two further pieces analyze the "high-debt, high-tax, low-growth doom loop," warning that without curbing public spending Britain risks tipping into a full-blown debt crisis. Despite the range of authors and framings - economic, market-based, ideological, and geopolitical - all the articles share a common frame: blaming excessive government spending and tax increases, consistently calling for cuts and fiscal discipline, and sharply criticizing any inclination within government or Labour's ranks to favor further state intervention over debt reduction.
