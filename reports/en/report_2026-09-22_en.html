CDU's electoral collapse and AfD rise in German state elections

⌄

Coverage of the CDU's electoral collapse and the AfD's rise diverges sharply in emphasis and diagnosis. The Daily Telegraph offers the starkest personal indictment, with one article calling Merz's performance in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern an outright disaster driven by his own weak leadership and communication, while a companion piece broadens the frame to Germany's car industry crisis, arguing that government failures on energy costs and Chinese competition - not just Merz's unpopularity - hand the AfD political ammunition. The Guardian takes a more ideological line, arguing that Merz's austerity and anti-immigration strategy itself is the problem, since only tackling economic insecurity, not chasing the far right rhetorically, can blunt its appeal. The Los Angeles Times and The Wall Street Journal instead focus on Merz's resilience despite the losses, portraying his push for pension and healthcare reform as evidence of political survival amid what the Journal calls a broader European "transformation of the party system." Süddeutsche Zeitung provides the most granular domestic view across several pieces: one notes that the AfD's strong Mecklenburg-Vorpommern result leaves it without a viable governing partner even as the CDU's weak campaign enabled its rise; another describes an internal "chancellor drama" in which Merz had little choice but to act preemptively against unrest in his own party; a third warns that political instability, including mixed signals around Berlin's Olympic bid, is damaging Germany's international standing. Die Welt frames the crisis structurally, with one commentary arguing that the CDU, SPD and Greens have fused into an indistinct centrist bloc since the Merkel era, pushing voters toward the AfD and Linkspartei alike, while another Welt piece turns to the SPD's own disunity after its mixed showing. Süddeutsche Zeitung and Die Welt also converge on Berlin's likely red-green-red coalition, both raising concerns about the Left party's tolerance of antisemitic and anti-Israel currents among some supporters, though Die Welt is considerably blunter, declaring the party has "no credibility whatsoever" on the issue, while Süddeutsche Zeitung frames it as a more measured warning. Finally, The Washington Post stands apart by locating the deeper cause in the liberal establishment's long-standing failure to recognize East Germany's distinct political and cultural identity, which it says has fueled support for both the AfD and the post-Communist left.

Russian parliamentary election and Putin's grip on power

⌄

The Washington Post ties the political critique to operational reality, framing the vote as "political theatre" while noting Ukraine underscored its point with a live attack on Russian energy infrastructure, contrasting the fabricated mandate with continued military capability on the ground.
